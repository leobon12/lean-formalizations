**Aux. lemma 52** (Global determination from measurable local restarts). *Let $X$ be a continuous process with values in a Polish space, with its usual natural filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t)$ and a stopping lifetime $T$. Suppose the stopped history has a continuous limit at every finite lifetime endpoint under consideration. Let $H$ be a random element of a standard Borel space, and assume that $X_0$ is $\sigma(H)$-measurable. A history includes its stopping time: write*

$$
\mathcal{X}_\tau = (\tau, (X_{q\wedge\tau})_{q\in\mathbb{Q}_+}).
$$

*Assume the following local extension property. Whenever $\tau \le T$ is an $\mathcal{F}_t$-stopping time and $\mathcal{X}_\tau$ is $\sigma(H)$-measurable, for each finite $N$ with $\tau \le T \wedge N$ there is an $\mathcal{F}_t$-stopping time $\sigma \le T \wedge N$ such that*

$$
\sigma \ge \tau, \qquad \sigma > \tau \text{ on } \{\tau < T \wedge N\}, \qquad \mathcal{X}_\sigma \in \sigma(H). \tag{LG1}
$$

*Then $\mathcal{X}_{T\wedge N}$ is $\sigma(H)$-measurable for every finite $N$. In particular the full process before $T$, and any continuous finite terminal value, are determined by $H$.*

<a id="aux-52-proof"></a>
*Proof.* Fix $N$. Let $\mathcal{S}$ be the set of stopping times $\tau \le T \wedge N$ whose stopped histories are $\sigma(H)$-measurable. It contains zero by the assumption on the initial state. It is closed under finite maxima: from the two recorded stopping times one knows which of their recorded histories is the longer one. It is also closed under increasing limits. Indeed, if $\tau_n \uparrow \tau$, then $\tau$ is a stopping time, and continuity gives $X_{q\wedge\tau_n} \to X_{q\wedge\tau}$ for every rational $q$. The limit time and all these coordinates are measurable with respect to $H$. The stated endpoint assumption is used only if $\tau = T < \infty$.

Set $a = \sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}} \mathbb{E}\tau \le N$ and choose $\tau_n \in \mathcal{S}$ with $\mathbb{E}\tau_n \to a$. Replace this sequence by its successive finite maxima. Its increasing limit $\tau_*$ belongs to $\mathcal{S}$ and has $\mathbb{E}\tau_* = a$. If $\tau \in \mathcal{S}$, its maximum with $\tau_*$ also belongs to $\mathcal{S}$, so

$$
0 \le \mathbb{E}[(\tau - \tau_*)^+] = \mathbb{E}[\tau \vee \tau_*] - a \le 0.
$$

Thus $\tau_*$ is an essential upper bound for every member of $\mathcal{S}$. Apply (LG1) at $\tau_*$. The resulting $\sigma$ belongs to $\mathcal{S}$ and is strictly larger on $\{\tau_* < T \wedge N\}$. The essential-upper-bound property forces that event to have probability zero. **Aux. lemma 57** (The hydrodynamic derivative argument on an untouched interval). *Let $K \subset \overline{\mathbb{H}}$ be bounded and closed, let $D = \mathbb{H}\setminus K$ be simply connected, and suppose that $K$ is the closure of $K \cap \mathbb{H}$ (irrelevant extra real points may instead be discarded). **Aux. lemma 68** (Minimal contacts of two simple arcs). *Let $\alpha : [0, A] \to X$ and $\beta : [0, B] \to X$ be continuous injective maps into a Hausdorff space, with $A, B < \infty$. Suppose their ranges meet. Write*

$$
C = \{(s, t) \in [0, A] \times [0, B] : \alpha(s) = \beta(t)\}.
$$

*Call $(s, t) \in C$ minimal if there is no distinct $(u, v) \in C$ with $u \le s$ and $v \le t$, and let $P$ be the set of minimal contacts. Then the following assertions hold.*

1. *$P$ is a nonempty compact set. Every point of $C$ dominates a point of $P$ in the coordinatewise order.*
2. *If $(a, b), (c, d) \in P$ are distinct and $a < c$, then $b > d$. Consequently the two coordinate projections of $P$ are homeomorphisms onto their images, and the correspondence between those images is strictly decreasing.*
3. *The first intersection points seen in the two traversal orders agree if and only if $P$ consists of one point.*
4. *Suppose $(a, b), (c, d) \in P$, $a < c$, and the first-coordinate projection of $P$ contains no point in $(a, c)$. Then*

   $$
   \alpha([a, c]) \cap \beta([d, b]) = \{\alpha(a), \alpha(c)\}.
   $$

   *Moreover, $\alpha([0, a])$ and $\beta([0, d])$ are disjoint; the first of these prefixes meets $\beta([d, b])$ only at $\alpha(a)$, and the second meets $\alpha([a, c])$ only at $\alpha(c)$.*
5. *If the first-coordinate projection of $P$ contains a nondegenerate interval $[a, c]$, then $\alpha([a, c])$ is a common subarc traversed by $\beta$ in the opposite order.*

*In particular, if the two first intersection points differ, then either there is an oppositely traversed common nondegenerate subarc, or there are two adjacent minimal contacts as in part 4. In the plane, the two subarcs in part 4 form a Jordan curve.*

<a id="aux-68-proof"></a>
*Proof.* The diagonal in $X \times X$ is closed, so $C$ is compact. For $(s, t) \in C$, minimize $u + v$ on the nonempty compact set $C \cap ([0, s] \times [0, t])$. A minimizer cannot strictly dominate another contact and is therefore in $P$. This proves both nonemptiness and the domination assertion.

Injectivity has the following useful consequence: two distinct contacts have different first coordinates and different second coordinates. Thus a distinct contact dominated by another is strictly smaller in both coordinates. If $(s_n, t_n) \in P$ converges to $(s, t) \in C$ and $(u, v) \in C$ strictly dominates it from below, then $u < s$ and $v < t$. These strict inequalities hold with $(s_n, t_n)$ in place of $(s, t)$ for all sufficiently large $n$, contradicting minimality. Hence $P$ is closed and compact.

<!-- page 273 -->
For distinct $(a, b), (c, d) \in P$ with $a < c$, the inequality $b \le d$ would make $(a, b)$ dominate $(c, d)$ from below. Therefore $b > d$. The coordinate projections are continuous injections from a compact space into a Hausdorff space, hence homeomorphisms onto their images.

Let $s_*$ and $t_*$ be the least first and second coordinates of $C$. The contacts with these coordinates belong to $P$. If their points agree, injectivity identifies both contacts with $(s_*, t_*)$, which dominates every contact and is the only point of $P$. Conversely, if $P$ is a singleton, the domination assertion identifies its coordinates with $s_*$ and $t_*$.

Consider part 4. Any additional contact between the two indicated subarcs has coordinates $(u, v)$ strictly inside $(a, c) \times (d, b)$: contacts on the four edges are excluded by injectivity. Choose a minimal contact $(r, w)$ dominated by $(u, v)$. If $r \le a$, then $w \le v < b$, so $(r, w)$ contradicts minimality of $(a, b)$. If $w \le d$, then $r \le u < c$, so it contradicts minimality of $(c, d)$. Thus $a < r < c$ and $d < w < b$, contrary to the assumed gap in the projection of $P$. A contact between the two initial prefixes would similarly dominate $(a, b)$. A contact between $\alpha([0, a])$ and $\beta([d, b])$ other than $(a, b)$ would dominate $(a, b)$; the analogous assertion at $(c, d)$ is the same. This proves all the separation assertions.

For part 5, the homeomorphism supplied by part 2 restricts to a continuous strictly decreasing map on $[a, c]$. Its image is the interval between its endpoint values. Equality of the two curves at the corresponding parameters is precisely the claimed reverse traversal.

Finally, the first-coordinate projection of $P$ is a nonempty compact subset of the real line. If it has at least two points and is the whole interval between its extreme points, part 5 applies. Otherwise any component of its complement between those extreme points is an open interval with both endpoints in the projection, and part 4 applies. In the planar case, gluing the two injective subarcs of part 4 along their two distinct endpoints gives a continuous injection of the circle. Its image is a Jordan curve by definition. $\square$
