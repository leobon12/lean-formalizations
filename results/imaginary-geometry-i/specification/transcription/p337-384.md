**Aux. lemma 100** (Positive-time absolute continuity for the Jacobi entrance). *Let $a, b, \kappa > 0$, and let $U$ be the $[0, 1]$-valued Jacobi diffusion driven by a Brownian motion $\mathcal{B}$:*

$$ dU_v = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2} U_v (1 - U_v)}\, d\mathcal{B}_v + \frac{1}{2}\{a(1 - U_v) - b U_v\}\, dv. \tag{J1} $$

*For every starting state $x \in [0, 1]$ and every $v > 0$, the law of $U_v$ is absolutely continuous with respect to*

$$ \pi(dy) = \frac{y^{\alpha-1}(1 - y)^{\beta-1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)}\, dy, \qquad \alpha = \frac{2a}{\kappa}, \qquad \beta = \frac{2b}{\kappa}. \tag{J2} $$

*In particular it has no atom at either endpoint, even when the starting state is an endpoint. The transition density has a continuous version $p_v(x, y)$ for $v > 0$ and $x, y \in [0, 1]$. No strict-positivity assertion for this density is needed below.*

<a id="aux-100-proof"></a>
*Proof.* Only the existence of the diffusion solving (J1), already supplied by the one-dimensional construction, is used. The proof does not use any SLE trace theorem. Write $w(y) = y^{\alpha-1}(1 - y)^{\beta-1}$ and $\gamma = \alpha + \beta$. Its polynomial generator is

$$ \mathcal{L} f = \frac{\kappa}{4}\{y(1 - y) f'' + (\alpha - \gamma y) f'\} = \frac{\kappa}{4w}\big(y(1 - y) w f'\big)'. \tag{J3} $$

For $n \ge 0$ define the Rodrigues polynomial

$$ J_n(y) = \frac{(-1)^n}{n!\, w(y)} \frac{d^n}{dy^n}\big(y^{n+\alpha-1}(1 - y)^{n+\beta-1}\big), \qquad J_0 = 1. \tag{J4} $$

Leibniz' rule shows that this is a polynomial of degree $n$. For $n \ge 1$ its leading coefficient is

$$ L_n = \frac{(2n + \gamma - 2)(2n + \gamma - 3) \cdots (n + \gamma - 1)}{n!} > 0. \tag{J5} $$

Indeed, the coefficient obtained from Leibniz' rule is the Vandermonde sum for falling factorials. This identity follows by differentiating $z^{A+B} = z^A z^B$ $n$ times at $z = 1$, with $A = n + \alpha - 1$ and $B = n + \beta - 1$.

Integration by parts $n$ times gives $\int_0^1 J_n(y) q(y) w(y)\, dy = 0$ for every polynomial $q$ of degree less than $n$. All boundary terms vanish: derivatives of order at most $n - 1$ of the expression inside parentheses in (J4) tend to zero at the endpoints, with powers at least $\alpha$ and $\beta$, respectively. One may first integrate on $[\varepsilon, 1 - \varepsilon]$ and pass to the limit;

<!-- page 345 -->

the remaining endpoint powers are integrable because $\alpha, \beta > 0$. The same integration by parts with $q = J_n$ gives the exact positive squared norm

$$ h_n := \int_0^1 J_n(y)^2 w(y)\, dy = L_n\, \mathrm{B}(n + \alpha, n + \beta), \quad n \ge 1, \qquad h_0 = \mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta). \tag{J6} $$

Thus $P_n = J_n \sqrt{\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)/h_n}$ are orthonormal in $L^2(\pi)$, with $P_0 = 1$. Their nonzero leading coefficients show that they span all polynomials. Polynomials are uniformly dense in $C([0, 1])$ and hence dense in $L^2(\pi)$.

Equation (J3) and one integration by parts make $\mathcal{L}$ symmetric on polynomials: the boundary term contains $y^\alpha(1 - y)^\beta$, which tends to zero at both ends. The coefficient of $y^n$ in $\mathcal{L} y^n$ is $-\lambda_n$, where

$$ \lambda_n = \frac{\kappa}{4} n(n - 1) + \frac{a + b}{2} n. \tag{J7} $$

Consequently $\mathcal{L} P_n + \lambda_n P_n$ has degree less than $n$ and is orthogonal to every polynomial of degree less than $n$, by symmetry. It is therefore zero.

We give an elementary growth bound sufficient to justify the spectral series. Let $M = \max(\alpha, \beta)$. The finite Leibniz sum in (J4), whose falling-factorial factors are all positive before its alternating signs, gives

$$ \sup_{0 \le y \le 1} |J_n(y)| \le \frac{(n + M)^n}{n!}. \tag{J8} $$

For $n \ge 2$, every factor in the numerator of (J5) is at least $n/2$, whereas $n! \le n^n$, so $L_n \ge 2^{-n}$. On $[1/3, 2/3]$, $y(1 - y) \ge 2/9$. Hence

$$ \mathrm{B}(n + \alpha, n + \beta) = \int_0^1 [y(1 - y)]^n w(y)\, dy \ge c_0 (2/9)^n, \quad c_0 = \int_{1/3}^{2/3} w(y)\, dy > 0. $$

Together with (J6), this gives $h_n \ge c_0 9^{-n}$. Adjusting a constant for $n = 0, 1$, (J8) proves

$$ \|P_n\|_\infty \le \exp\big(C(n + 1)\log(n + 2)\big) \tag{J9} $$

for some finite $C$ depending only on $\alpha, \beta$. Since $\lambda_n$ grows quadratically, for every $\tau > 0$ the series

$$ p_v(x, y) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\lambda_n v} P_n(x) P_n(y) \tag{J10} $$

converges absolutely and uniformly on $[\tau, \infty) \times [0, 1]^2$. It defines a continuous, bounded function there.

It remains to identify this analytic series with the actual transition law, including when $x = 0$ or $1$. Ito's formula and $\mathcal{L} P_n = -\lambda_n P_n$ give

$$ \mathbb{E}_x[P_n(U_v)] = e^{-\lambda_n v} P_n(x). \tag{J11} $$

<!-- page 346 -->

The stochastic integral is a true martingale on each finite interval: the polynomial and its derivative are bounded on $[0, 1]$, as is the diffusion coefficient in (J1). This proof of (J11) applies equally at the endpoints and presupposes no transition density.

For fixed $x$ and $v > 0$, (J10) defines a finite signed measure $p_v(x, y)\pi(dy)$. Uniform convergence and orthonormality show that its integral against each $P_n$ equals the right side of (J11). It therefore has the same integral as the transition law against every polynomial. Uniform polynomial approximation on $[0, 1]$ identifies the two finite measures. In particular $p_v(x, \cdot)\pi$ is a probability measure and has no endpoint atoms, since $\pi$ has none. Its density is nonnegative: otherwise continuity and the full support of $\pi$ would give a set of negative mass. This proves the lemma. $\square$

*Consequence for the future diffusion law.* The measure $\pi$ is invariant. This follows either by integrating (J10), or by integrating (J11) and using polynomial approximation. If the initial law of a Markov solution is $\mu \ll \pi$, its entire future path law is absolutely continuous with respect to that of the stationary solution. Indeed, both use the same conditional future kernel given $U_0 = x$, and integrating a zero-probability future event against $\pi$ gives a kernel which vanishes for $\pi$-almost every $x$, hence also for $\mu$-almost every $x$. By the lemma, after any positive deterministic amount of Jacobi time this applies to a solution started from any specified point of $[0, 1]$. The same conclusion holds for a mixture of initial states. This is the one-way absolute continuity needed to transfer a probability-one future-path property from the stationary entrance construction; mutual absolute continuity or a strictly positive heat kernel is unnecessary.

**Aux. lemma 101** (Finite angular clock on every infinite one-sided phase). *Let $\kappa > 0$ and consider integrated force-point equations with at least one mark on each side and positive nearest-group cross-gap at a stopping time $\sigma$. Retain the force labels through instantaneous admissible collisions. Stop on a return to the moving midpoint or any earlier prescribed event. On an interval during which the driver stays on one side of that midpoint, the angular clock*

$$ u(t) = \int_\sigma^t D_v^{-2}\, dv, \qquad D = V^{1,R} - V^{1,L}, $$

*has finite terminal value almost surely. No restriction on the two total weights is needed. The opposite group can therefore be deleted by a change of driving law valid on the entire stopped interval, including its infinite-duration event. If deleting that group leaves a configuration covered by the continuity and transience induction hypothesis, those properties transfer to this interval.*

<a id="aux-101-proof"></a>
*Proof.* Reflect if necessary so the driver stays to the left of the midpoint. Put $L = W - V^{1,L}$, $R = V^{1,R} - W$, $D = L + R$, and $U = L/D \in [0, 1/2]$. Write

$$ s = \sum_i \rho_i^L, \quad q = \sum_i \rho_i^R, \quad \Delta_i^L = V^{1,L} - V^{i,L}, \quad \Delta_i^R = V^{i,R} - V^{1,R}. $$

<!-- page 347 -->

Each spread is nonnegative and decreasing, with

$$ d\Delta_i^L = -\frac{2\Delta_i^L}{L(L + \Delta_i^L)}\, dt, \qquad d\Delta_i^R = -\frac{2\Delta_i^R}{R(R + \Delta_i^R)}\, dt. \tag{P1} $$

The force equations give, in the angular clock,

$$ dU = \sqrt{\kappa}\, d\beta + \left(\frac{2 + s}{U} - \frac{2 + q}{1 - U}\right) du + dA, \tag{P2} $$

where the finite-variation error is exactly

$$ dA = \frac{1}{2D}\left(\sum_i \rho_i^L\, d\Delta_i^L - \sum_i \rho_i^R\, d\Delta_i^R\right). \tag{P3} $$

In particular

$$ \operatorname{TV}(A) \le \frac{\sum_i |\rho_i^L| \Delta_i^L(\sigma) + \sum_i |\rho_i^R| \Delta_i^R(\sigma)}{2D_\sigma} < \infty, \tag{P4} $$

since $D$ is increasing. These are identities for the integrated equations. On every finite physical interval their singular integrals are finite, and admissible collision times have zero Lebesgue measure. The displayed identities therefore continue through such collisions; no reflection local-time term is added to $A$.

Suppose for contradiction that the terminal angular clock is infinite. The time-changed martingale $\beta$ is then a Brownian motion for all $u \ge 0$. One may extend it by an independent Brownian motion after a finite angular lifetime when defining a common full-clock Brownian motion; only the infinite-lifetime event is at issue below. On every finite angular interval of that event the corresponding physical time is finite, so (P2) is integrable throughout that interval.

If $2 + s \ge 0$, the drift in (P2) is bounded below by $-C$, where $C = 2(2 + q)_+$. Whenever

$$ \sqrt{\kappa}\,[\beta(n + 1) - \beta(n)] > C + 1, $$

integrating (P2) over $[n, n + 1]$ and using $0 \le U \le 1/2$ forces $\operatorname{TV}(A; [n, n + 1]) > 1/2$. Infinitely many of these Brownian increments occur almost surely, since the increments are independent and their common success probability is positive. This contradicts (P4). If $2 + s < 0$, the same drift is bounded above by $C = 2(-2 - q)_+$. Infinitely many Brownian increments less than $-(C + 1)/\sqrt{\kappa}$ now force the same variation cost in order to keep $U$ nonnegative. This is again impossible. Thus the angular clock is finite in every case.

Let $B = \sum_i |\rho_i^R|$. Deleting the entire right group changes the driving drift by at most $B/R \le 2B/D$, so its Girsanov energy on a phase with terminal angular clock less than $m$ is bounded by

$$ \int_\sigma^\tau \frac{|e_t|^2}{\kappa}\, dt \le \frac{4B^2 m}{\kappa}. \tag{P5} $$

Stop additionally when the angular clock reaches $m$. The bounded-energy exponential and its reciprocal are uniformly integrable and have strictly positive terminal values. Applying Girsanov to the integrated equations, retaining the deleted marks as passive coordinates for the stopping rule, identifies the lower-count law by its uniqueness. The unaltered-side collision integrals and continuation threshold are preserved. Hence the two entire stopped driving laws are equivalent, also at an infinite terminal physical time. Taking the union over positive integers $m$ covers the finite-angular-clock event just proved. This proves the final transfer assertion. $\square$

**Aux. lemma 102** (Finite total angular clock with a nonpositive endpoint parameter). *Consider integrated force-point equations with $\kappa > 0$, at least one group on each side, and positive initial cross-gap. Let the total side weights be $s, q$. If $s \le -2$ or $q \le -2$, then the total angular clock $\int D_t^{-2} dt$ until the continuation threshold, or any earlier prescribed stopping rule, is finite almost surely. This is a statement about the force-point equations and assumes no trace theorem.*

<a id="aux-102-proof"></a>
*Proof.* Retain all force labels in the integrated equations through admissible collisions and mergers. Equations (P1)–(P4) hold with $U = L/D \in [0, 1]$ on the entire lifetime. In particular the error $A$ has finite total variation globally; choosing a last merger time is unnecessary. Put $a = 2 + s$ and $b = 2 + q$. Reflect if necessary so that $a \le 0$. We show that the total angular clock is finite. If $b \ge 0$, the drift $a/U - b/(1 - U)$ is nonpositive throughout $(0, 1)$. Every Brownian unit increment smaller than $-2/\sqrt{\kappa}$ forces variation of $A$ at least one if $U$ remains in $[0, 1]$. Infinitely many such increments occur on an infinite Brownian clock, contradicting finite variation.

Suppose now that $b < 0$. Fix $\varepsilon = 1/64$. At each integer angular time $n$, choose one of the following two Brownian tube events, according to the current value of $U(n)$. If $U(n) \le 3/4$, put $C_- = 8|b|$ and require

$$ \sup_{0 \le v \le 1} \big|\sqrt{\kappa}\,[\beta(n + v) - \beta(n)] + (C_- + 2)v\big| < \varepsilon. \tag{P6} $$

If also $\operatorname{TV}(A; [n, n + 1]) < \varepsilon$, the drift bound $a/U - b/(1 - U) \le C_-$ while $U \le 7/8$ gives

$$ U(n + v) \le U(n) - 2v + 2\varepsilon. $$

It cannot first reach $7/8$, since $3/4 + 2\varepsilon < 7/8$; at $v = 1$ the same inequality would make it negative. Thus (P6), on a surviving unit interval, requires variation at least $\varepsilon$.

If $U(n) > 3/4$, put $C_+ = (8/5)|a|$ and require instead

$$ \sup_{0 \le v \le 1} \big|\sqrt{\kappa}\,[\beta(n + v) - \beta(n)] - (C_+ + 2)v\big| < \varepsilon. \tag{P7} $$

While $U \ge 5/8$, the drift is at least $-C_+$. If the variation of $A$ is less than $\varepsilon$, this gives $U(n + v) \ge U(n) + 2v - 2\varepsilon$. It cannot first reach $5/8$, and at $v = 1$ it would exceed one. Thus this tube event has the same variation cost.

Each fixed Brownian tube in (P6)–(P7) has strictly positive probability. For example, translation by its linear function has a strictly positive Gaussian density on the unit-time Brownian path space, and Brownian motion has positive probability to stay in a fixed interval about zero for one unit of time. The smaller of the two probabilities is a constant $p > 0$. The choice at time $n$ uses only the current adapted state, so its conditional success probability is at least $p$. Iteration of conditional expectations bounds the probability of no success in any next $k$ trials by $(1 - p)^k$. Taking a countable union over starting integers shows that infinitely many selected tubes occur on a full infinite clock. Each would require variation at least $\varepsilon$ on a disjoint unit interval, contradicting (P4). As in the previous lemma, extend Brownian motion after a finite clock when defining the tube trials; the contradiction concerns the event of infinite angular lifetime. After a finite clock has ended, define the tube choice arbitrarily, for example always by the negative tube, so all subsequent conditional trials are well defined without assigning a value to a nonexistent $U(n)$. This proves finiteness of the total angular clock whenever $a \le 0$ or $b \le 0$. $\square$

**Aux. lemma 117** (The martingale converse away from critical colliding weights). *Let $k > 0$ and let a continuous Loewner trace have a continuous driver in its capacity parametrization on $[0, T)$, where $T > 0$ is a stopping lifetime in a usual filtration. *Proof.* Put $X = 2/\sqrt{k} - \sqrt{k}/2$ and $f_t = g_t - W_t$. The analytic function whose imaginary part is the canonical mean is

$$ \begin{aligned} H_t(z) = {} & \frac{i\pi}{\sqrt{k}}\left(1 + \sum_{j \text{ on the right}} r_j\right) - \frac{2}{\sqrt{k}} \log f_t(z) \\ & - \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \sum_j r_j \log(g_t(z) - V_t^j) - X \log g_t'(z). \end{aligned} \tag{NC9.1} $$

is a continuous finite-variation process whose measure is supported on $\{V^j = W\}$. The reciprocal integral is absolutely finite on compact intervals, because its absolute integral is at most the variation of $V^j$ there.

$$ dQ_t = k\,dt, \qquad dA_t = \mu_t dt - \frac{1}{2}\sum_j r_j dI_t^j. \tag{NC9.4} $$

At a positive collision time the left and right groups are separated. Indeed their continuous monotone coordinates start ordered. Its gap $Y \ge 0$ has quadratic variation $k\,dt$. Its reciprocal integrability gives

$$ \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_s^t \mathbf{1}_{\{0 < Y_u < \varepsilon\}}\, d\langle Y \rangle_u \le k \int_s^t \frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{0 < Y_u < \varepsilon\}}}{Y_u}\, du \longrightarrow 0. $$

$$ (1 + r/2)\, dI = 0. \tag{NC9.5} $$

The Brownian term has no contribution on the null collision set, and every absolutely continuous term has none either. For a right group the signs reverse but the same equation results. treats all positive collisions. The measures have no atoms, so time zero adds nothing. Hence every $I^j$ vanishes.
