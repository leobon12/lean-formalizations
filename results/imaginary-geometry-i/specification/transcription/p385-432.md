**Aux. lemma 133** (Uniform shielding from a first-encounter rule). *Let $B^+ = \{z : |z| < 1, \operatorname{Im} z > 0\}$, and let $\gamma_i : [0, 1] \to \overline{B^+}$, $1 \le i \le m$, be injective continuous paths with $\gamma_i(0) = 0$. *Let $\mathcal{A}$ be any family of continuous paths $\alpha : [0, T_\alpha] \to \overline{\mathbb{H}}$ with $|\alpha(0)| \ge 1$. $$ \gamma_i^{-1}(\alpha(s_i)) = \min\{t : \gamma_i(t) \in \alpha([0, T_\alpha])\}. $$

*Then there exists $\delta > 0$, depending only on the finite family $(\gamma_i)$, such that every path in $\mathcal{A}$ avoids $B(0, \delta)$. In particular, $0$ is outside the closure of the union of their ranges.*

<a id="aux-133-proof"></a>
*Proof.* We first explain how to construct an arbitrarily small crosscut from compact subarcs of the $\gamma_i$, avoiding $0$. Choose an intersection point between every consecutive pair of paths, a point of $\gamma_1$ on the negative real axis, and a point of $\gamma_m$ on the positive real axis. These points can be chosen arbitrarily close to $0$. Injectivity and compactness imply that their parameters on each participating path tend to $0$ as the points tend to $0$. The portions of the paths joining successive chosen points therefore lie in an arbitrarily small ball. Their concatenation is a path from a negative to a positive real point, is made of finitely many compact subarcs, and avoids $0$.

<!-- page 430 -->
The image of a path contains a simple arc between its endpoints. Choose one. On that arc, take the portion from its last visit to the negative real axis before its first visit to the positive real axis. Its interior is in $B^+$, since it avoids $0$ and has no other real-axis visits. This is the required crosscut $C$. The crosscut and the real interval joining its endpoints form a Jordan curve. Write $V(C)$ for the region between them in $B^+$. Because $C$ is compact and avoids $0$, this region contains $B^+ \cap B(0, r)$ for some $r > 0$.

Construct $m + 1$ such crosscuts $C_1, \ldots, C_{m+1}$ recursively, with $C_1 \subset B(0, 1/2)$ and each later crosscut inside the region bounded by its predecessor. Make the recursive choice sufficiently small that, for every $i$ and $j \ge 2$, the whole initial segment of $\gamma_i$ up to the last parameter used in constructing $C_j$ lies in a ball around $0$ whose closure is disjoint from $C_{j-1}$ and whose intersection with $B^+$ is contained in $V(C_{j-1})$. This is possible by the preceding construction and continuity at $0$. In particular, the crosscuts are pairwise disjoint.

Choose $\delta > 0$ so that $B^+ \cap B(0, \delta) \subset V(C_{m+1})$ and $\overline{B(0, \delta)}$ is disjoint from $C_{m+1}$. Suppose a path $\alpha \in \mathcal{A}$ reaches this ball. Its initial point lies outside $C_1$ and all the smaller crosscuts. The Jordan separation theorem in the closed upper half-plane gives successive first hitting times $\sigma_1 < \cdots < \sigma_{m+1}$ of the crosscuts. The same conclusion holds when $\alpha$ touches the real boundary: to enter the real interval behind a crosscut it must pass one of that crosscut's endpoints. For each $j$ choose $i_j$ such that $\alpha(\sigma_j)$ belongs to one of the compact subarcs of $\gamma_{i_j}$ used in constructing $C_j$. Such a choice exists because those subarcs cover $C_j$. In particular, the parameter of $\alpha(\sigma_j)$ on $\gamma_{i_j}$ is no larger than the last parameter of that path used in constructing $C_j$.

We claim that the $i_j$ are distinct. Suppose $i_j = i_k = i$ with $j < k$. The first time $s_i$ that $\alpha$ meets $\gamma_i$ satisfies $s_i \le \sigma_j$. By the assumed first-encounter rule, the parameter of $\alpha(s_i)$ on $\gamma_i$ is at most the parameter of $\alpha(\sigma_k)$. By the choice of $i_k$, this parameter is no larger than the last parameter of $\gamma_i$ used in constructing $C_k$. The recursive construction therefore places this entire initial segment strictly behind $C_{k-1}$, in a ball disjoint from that crosscut. Thus $\alpha$ must already have crossed $C_{k-1}$ before time $s_i$. This contradicts $s_i \le \sigma_j \le \sigma_{k-1}$. There would therefore be $m + 1$ distinct indices in $\{1, \ldots, m\}$, a contradiction. $\square$

*Scope.* The first-encounter rule is stronger than the assertion that two paths cross at most once. Two touching arcs can bound a sequence of adjacent pockets, and a third continuous path can pass through their successive touching points without crossing either arc. Consequently, a proof of the first-encounter rule, with the correct angle and prime-end conventions, is required before this lemma can be applied to the flow-line shield of Proposition 7.33.
