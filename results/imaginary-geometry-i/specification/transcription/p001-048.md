## Foundations and conventions

**Dirichlet normalization and the field.** Let $D \subset \mathbb{C}$ have a nontrivial Dirichlet Green function. For $f, g \in C_c^\infty(D)$ set

$$ (f,g)_\nabla = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_D \nabla f \cdot \nabla g \, dx, \qquad H(D) = \overline{C_c^\infty(D)}^{\|\cdot\|_\nabla}. $$

The zero-boundary Gaussian free field is the distributional realization of the isonormal Gaussian process on $H(D)$. Equivalently, for an orthonormal basis $(\varphi_n)$ and independent standard normal variables $(\alpha_n)$, its formal expansion is

$$ h_0 = \sum_n \alpha_n \varphi_n. \tag{1.3} $$

This expansion denotes the Gaussian distribution-valued construction of Section 3, not a pointwise-defined function. A field with deterministic harmonic mean $m$ is $h = h_0 + m$. Whenever bounded piecewise-constant boundary data are specified in $\mathbb{H}$ or in the strip, their harmonic extension means the bounded harmonic extension in that specified chart. Other means must be specified explicitly; an unbounded harmonic function with zero boundary trace is not silently added.

**Parameters and angle labels.** For any parameter $a > 0$, put

$$ \lambda(a) = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{a}}, \qquad \chi(a) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{a}} - \frac{\sqrt{a}}{2}. $$

In a flow–counterflow pair the conventions are

$$ 0 < \kappa < 4, \quad \kappa' = \frac{16}{\kappa}, \quad \lambda = \lambda(\kappa), \quad \lambda' = \lambda(\kappa') = \frac{\pi\sqrt{\kappa}}{4}, \quad \chi = \chi(\kappa) > 0. $$

Thus $\chi(\kappa') = -\chi$ and $\lambda - \lambda' = \pi\chi/2$. A flow line of $h$ with real angle $\theta$ means the flow line coupled to $h + \theta\chi$. Angles and derivative arguments retain their real lifts; they are not reduced modulo $2\pi$. For a distribution-valued GFF, flow lines are defined by the coupling of Theorem 1.1 and its field-determination theorem, not by a pointwise vector-field ordinary differential equation.

**Conformal coordinate and derivative lift.** Let $\psi : \widetilde{D} \to D$ be conformal and let $L_\psi$ be the chosen analytic logarithm of $\psi'$. The field in the new coordinate is

$$ (D, h) \longmapsto (\widetilde{D}, h \circ \psi - \chi \operatorname{Im} L_\psi). \tag{1.4} $$

This is the coordinate rule also referred to as Figure 1.6. The inverse lift is $L_{\psi^{-1}} = -L_\psi \circ \psi^{-1}$. For compositions use the analytic chain-rule lift inherited from these choices. A change by $2\pi i n$ changes the actual lifted heights; it cannot be made independently on two sides of a comparison.

Boundary markings used in conformal maps are prime ends. Their Euclidean realizations require the stated boundary-extension hypotheses, automatic for Jordan domains. Continuous extension does not imply that different prime ends have different physical images.

The literal and centered counterflow conventions remain distinct:

$$ \operatorname{CF}_{\mathrm{lit}}(h) \text{ is coupled to } -h, \qquad \operatorname{CF}_d(h) = \operatorname{CF}_{\mathrm{lit}}(h + \pi\chi) \text{ is coupled to } -h - \pi\chi. $$

The full frame dictionary, including the infinity-source lift, is FD11.1–FD12.5 following Theorem 1.1. Its offset is part of the initial data and is transported unchanged by coordinate changes.

**Boundary-height notation.** The boundary-label convention in Figures 1.9–1.10 is the following. A label $a$ on a chosen oriented horizontal or vertical reference segment fixes its real height. On the adjoining displayed boundary arc the height changes by $\chi$ times the continuously lifted turning angle relative to that segment: a left quarter-turn adds $\pi\chi/2$ and a right quarter-turn subtracts $\pi\chi/2$. The colon-style labels in the retained figures prescribe this extension along the indicated arc; an ordinary label on a segment specifies that segment alone. For nonsmooth arcs the prescription denotes the corresponding harmonic mean obtained by the specified conformal coordinate and derivative lift, rather than pointwise values of the GFF. In the standard flow picture the side heights are $-\lambda' + \chi \cdot \text{winding}$ on the left and $\lambda' + \chi \cdot \text{winding}$ on the right, with the starting direction and lift fixed by the displayed reference frame.

**Loewner maps, hulls and clocks.** In $\mathbb{H}$, capacity time and a real continuous driving function $W$ define the Loewner maps by

$$ \partial_t g_t(z) = \frac{2}{g_t(z) - W_t}, \qquad g_0(z) = z. \tag{1.6} $$

The domain of $g_t$ is $\mathbb{H} \setminus K_t$, where $K_t$ is the filled Loewner hull; the maps have hydrodynamic normalization. Writing $f_t = g_t - W_t$ gives

$$ df_t(z) = \frac{2}{f_t(z)} \, dt - dW_t. \tag{1.7} $$

This centered map sends the current tip prime end to zero. The driver, hull and a continuous generating trace are separate objects. When a continuous trace exists, $K_t$ is the filling of its range through $t$, not generally its raw range. A relative exploration uses its own stated capacity clock and filtration; an identification with original time must be supplied by the relevant time-change argument.

**Ordered force words and integrated evolution.** An ordered finite force word consists of side-labelled positions and real weights

$$ x_{k,L} \le \cdots \le x_{1,L} \le 0 \le x_{1,R} \le \cdots \le x_{\ell,R}, \qquad \underline{\rho} = (\rho_L; \rho_R). $$

The side labels distinguish $0^-$ from $0^+$. An integrated $\mathrm{SLE}_a(\underline{\rho})$ driving evolution has continuous adapted coordinates $W, V^{i,q}$ and a Brownian motion $B$ in their joint filtration, with $W_0 = 0$, $V_0^{i,q} = x_{i,q}$, the corresponding side ordering, and

$$ W_t = \sqrt{a}\, B_t + \sum_{q \in \{L,R\}} \sum_i \int_0^t \frac{\rho_{i,q}}{W_s - V_s^{i,q}} \, ds, \tag{1.10} $$

$$ V_t^{i,q} = x_{i,q} + \int_0^t \frac{2}{V_s^{i,q} - W_s} \, ds. \tag{1.11} $$

Every reciprocal gap is absolutely integrable on compact time intervals before the lifetime. Each collision set $\{t : W_t = V_t^{i,q}\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero, and values assigned to the integrands on those sets do not affect the integrals. There are no additional singular finite-variation terms on collision sets. Coincident points on the same side are amalgamated by adding their weights. The continuation threshold is

$$ T_* = \inf\left\{ t : \text{ for some } q \in \{L,R\}, \ \sum_{i : V_t^{i,q} = W_t} \rho_{i,q} \le -2 \right\}. $$

An empty sum is zero and does not trigger the threshold. Only times before $T_*$ belong to the integrated evolution; a statement at a finite $T_*$ refers to its proved continuous limit. An initially inadmissible seed has lifetime zero. The detailed construction and weak uniqueness are Definition 2.1 and Theorem 2.2. Every surviving force mark is transported through a change of chart, including zero-weight marks when they define a stopping rule. Removing a mark because its weight is zero requires that its geometric role has also been discharged.

**Conditioning and foundational inputs.** Filtrations have their usual augmentation and must be named. Conditioning on an additional curve, a whole field, or only the driving history gives different conditional-law statements. A fresh conditional GFF has exactly the specified domain, mean and covariance; an outside-filled-hull kernel is not by itself a whole-field or raw-range kernel. The marked locality and optional-kernel results in Section 3 and the common mathematical interfaces remain part of the proofs.

The external foundation boundary consists of the precisely cited versions of stochastic integration and Girsanov, Brownian and Bessel/Jacobi diffusion theory, Gaussian disintegration, conformal mapping and boundary extension, planar separation, and ordinary SLE trace results. Parameter-four level-line inputs, parameter-eight ordinary-SLE inputs, and each additional imaginary-geometry input are named at their use. A formal development must implement or import those stated results with matching hypotheses. This mathematical specification is not a compiled Lean development.

## Main theorems

Throughout, a finite force word, its continuation lifetime, its complete retained coordinates, and its coupling field have the conventions fixed above. Theorem 1.1 and the frame dictionary specify the original optional coupling.

<a id="thm-1.2"></a>
**Theorem 1.2** (field determination). Fix $a > 0$ and a finite ordered force word. In every coupling satisfying the optional hull specification of Theorem 1.1, the entire capacity-parametrized trace, including its available finite continuation endpoint and its recorded lifetime, is a Borel function of the coupling field, up to a null set.

<a id="thm-1.3"></a>
**Theorem 1.3** (continuity and endpoint). The canonical integrated $\mathrm{SLE}_a(\underline{\rho})$ evolution has a continuous generating trace on $[0, T_*]$ when $T_* < \infty$ and on each finite interval when $T_* = \infty$. On the latter event,

$$ \lim_{t \to \infty} |\eta(t)| = \infty. $$

An initially inadmissible seed gives $T_* = 0$ and the singleton trace at the starting point.

**Proof interfaces for Theorems 1.2–1.3.** Corollary LP11 supplies both conclusions. For $0 < a < 4$ it uses the retained finite-force induction and determining graph. For $a > 4$ the continuous reference R.7, Lemma 7.25 and LP1–LP10 give the driving, trace and terminal assertions through separate dependency chains. At $a = 4$ the input is Wang–Wu [WW17, Theorems 1.1.2–1.1.3], with their coupling Theorem 1.1.1 and normalization $\lambda = \pi/2$. Here $\chi = 0$, so no formula dividing by $\chi$ is used.

<a id="thm-1.5"></a>
**Theorem 1.5** (constant-angle interaction). Let $0 < \kappa < 4$, let $h$ have finite piecewise-constant boundary data on $\mathbb{H}$, and let $\eta_\theta^x$ be its angle-$\theta$ flow line from $x \in \partial\mathbb{H}$. Fix $x_1 \ge x_2$. The contact assertions below concern interior contacts while the relevant continuations are available. Common terminal points are excluded from nonintersection conclusions.

(i) If $\theta_1 < \theta_2$, then $\eta_{\theta_1}^{x_1}$ stays to the right of $\eta_{\theta_2}^{x_2}$. If $\theta_2 - \theta_1 \ge \pi\kappa/(4 - \kappa)$, they do not intersect at an interior available contact, apart from a possible common starting point. Below this threshold, bouncing is an allowed local interaction.

(ii) If $\theta_1 = \theta_2$, then upon an interior available contact the curves merge and do not subsequently separate during their available common continuation.

(iii) If $x_1 > x_2$ and $\theta_2 < \theta_1 < \theta_2 + \pi$, then upon an interior available contact the first curve crosses the second and never crosses back. If $\theta_1 - \theta_2 \ge \pi\kappa/(4 - \kappa)$, there is no subsequent interior available contact. Below this threshold, subsequent bouncing is an allowed local interaction.

The statements permitting contact or bouncing do not assert that a pair with arbitrary boundary data actually meets. The proof is W5 and 7.12C–7.12D; the complete other-path conditional laws are CP2–CP3.

**Fan statements.** Fresh-tip exclusion and the zero-area fan conclusion retain the hypotheses of Proposition 7.33. They do not include an arbitrary-old-tip extension. Theorem OT1 gives the separate counterexample to that extension in the explicitly specified centered coupling. In particular no change to a literal background is made without the accompanying field and angle translation in FD11.3–FD11.4.

## Optional coupling and field frames

Unless an endpoint statement is made explicitly, coupling identities and explorations below are evaluated strictly before the continuation threshold. Continuity of the trace at the threshold is a separate assertion of Theorem 1.3; it does not by itself define a Loewner map or harmonic function on a swallowed set at that time. It turns out that the continuation threshold is infinite almost surely if and only if

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{j} \rho^{i,L} > -2 \quad \text{for all} \quad 1 \le j \le k \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{j} \rho^{i,R} > -2 \quad \text{for all} \quad 1 \le j \le \ell. $$

**Definition.** When $\kappa \in (0,4)$, we will refer to an $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ curve (if it exists) coupled with a GFF $h$ on $\mathbf{H}$ with boundary conditions as in Theorem 1.1 as a **flow line** of $h$. ## 2 The Schramm-Loewner evolution

### 2.1 Overview of $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa$

An $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa$ in $\mathbf{H}$ from $0$ to $\infty$ is defined by the random family of conformal maps $g_t$ obtained by solving the Loewner ODE (1.6) with $W = \sqrt{\kappa} B$ and $B$ a standard Brownian motion. Write $K_t := \{z \in \mathbf{H} : \tau(z) \le t\}$ where $\tau(z) = \sup\{t \ge 0 : \operatorname{Im}(g_t(z)) > 0\}$. Then $g_t$ is the unique conformal map from $\mathbf{H}_t := \mathbf{H} \setminus K_t$ to $\mathbf{H}$ satisfying $\lim_{|z| \to \infty} |g_t(z) - z| = 0$.

For $\kappa \ne 8$, Rohde and Schramm showed that there a.s. exists a curve $\eta$ (the so-called SLE *trace*); the case $\kappa = 8$ is due to Lawler, Schramm, and Werner [LSW04]. In every case there is a continuous trace such that for each $t \ge 0$ the domain $\mathbf{H}_t$ of $g_t$ is the unbounded connected component of $\mathbf{H} \setminus \eta([0,t])$, in which case the hull $\overline{K_t} \subseteq \overline{\mathbf{H}}$, whose complement in $\mathbf{H}$ is simply connected, is called the "filling" of $\eta([0,t])$ [RS05]. An $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa$ connecting boundary points $x$ and $y$ of an arbitrary simply connected Jordan domain can be constructed as the image of an $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa$ on $\mathbf{H}$ under a conformal transformation $\varphi \colon \mathbf{H} \to D$ sending $0$ to $x$ and $\infty$ to $y$. (The choice of $\varphi$ does not affect the law of this image path, since the law of $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa$ on $\mathbf{H}$ is scale invariant.)

### 2.2 Definition of $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\rho)$

The so-called $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ processes are an important variant of $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa$ in which one keeps track of additional marked points. Just as with regular $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa$, one constructs $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ using the Loewner equation except that the driving function $W$ is replaced with a solution to the SDE (1.10). The purpose of this section is to construct solutions to (1.10) in a careful and canonical way. We will not actually need to think about the Loewner evolution on the half plane for any of the discussion in this subsection. It will be enough for now to think about the Loewner evolution restricted to the real line.

<!-- page 19 -->

We first recall that the Bessel process of dimension $\delta > 0$, also written $\mathrm{BES}^\delta$, is in some sense a (non-negative) solution to the SDE

$$ dX_t = dB_t + \frac{\delta - 1}{2X_t} dt, \quad X_0 \ge 0 \tag{2.1} $$

where $B$ is a standard Brownian motion. A detailed construction of the Bessel processes appears, for example, in [RY99, Chapter XI]. We review a few of the basic facts here. When $\delta > 1$, (2.1) holds in the sense that $X$ is a.s. *instantaneously reflecting* at $0$ (i.e., the set of times for which $X_t = 0$ has Lebesgue measure zero) and a.s. satisfies

$$ X_t = X_0 + B_t + \int_0^t \frac{\delta - 1}{2X_s} ds, \quad X_0 \ge 0. \tag{2.2} $$

In particular, assuming $\delta > 1$, the integral in (2.2) is finite a.s. so that $X_t$ is a semimartingale. The solution is a strong solution in the sense of [RY99], which means that $X$ is adapted to the filtration generated by the Brownian motion $B$. The law of $X$ is determined by the fact that it is a solution to (2.1) away from times where $X_t = 0$, instantaneously reflecting where $X_t = 0$, and adapted to the filtration generated by $B$.

Regardless of $\delta$, standard SDE results imply that (2.2) has a unique solution up until the first time $t$ that $X_t = 0$. When $\delta < 1$, however, (2.2) cannot hold beyond times at which $X_t = 0$ without a so-called principal value correction, because the integral in (2.2) is almost surely infinite beyond such times (see [She09, Section 3.1] for additional discussion of this point). Bessel processes can be defined for all time whenever $\delta > 0$ but they are not semi-martingales when $\delta \in (0,1)$. For this paper, it turns out not to be necessary to consider settings that require a principal value correction. We may always assume that either $\delta > 1$ or that $\delta \le 1$ but we only consider the process up to the first time that $X$ reaches zero.

Fix a value $\rho > -2$ and write

$$ \delta = 1 + \frac{2(\rho + 2)}{\kappa}, $$

noting that $\delta > 1$. Given $x_0^R \ge 0$, let $X$ be an instantaneously reflecting solution to (2.1) with $X_0 = x_0^R/\sqrt{\kappa}$. We would like to define a pair $W$ and $V^R$ that solves the SDE (1.10) with $W_0 = 0$ and some fixed initial value $V_0^R = x_0^R \ge 0$. To motivate the definition, note that (1.10) formally implies that the difference $V^R - W$ solves the same SDE as $\sqrt{\kappa} X$, away from times where it is equal to zero. Thus it is natural to write

$$ \begin{aligned} V_t^R &= x_0^R + \int_0^t \frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa} X_s} ds, \\ W_t &= V_t^R - \sqrt{\kappa} X_t. \end{aligned} \tag{2.3} $$

The standard definition of (single-force-point) $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\rho)$ is the Loewner evolution driven by the process $W$ defined in (2.3).

<!-- page 20 -->

<a id="def-2.1"></a>
*Definition* 2.1. Fix $\kappa > 0$, finitely many real weights $\rho^{i,q}$, and ordered initial positions $x^{k,L} \le \cdots \le x^{1,L} \le 0 \le x^{1,R} \le \cdots \le x^{\ell,R}$. An $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ driving evolution is a collection of continuous adapted processes $W, V^{i,q}$, with $W_0 = 0$ and $V_0^{i,q} = x^{i,q}$, together with a Brownian motion $B$ in their joint filtration, satisfying the ordering

$$ V_t^{k,L} \le \cdots \le V_t^{1,L} \le W_t \le V_t^{1,R} \le \cdots \le V_t^{\ell,R} $$

and the integrated equations

$$ \begin{aligned} W_t &= \sqrt{\kappa} B_t + \sum_{q \in \{L,R\}} \sum_i \int_0^t \frac{\rho^{i,q}}{W_s - V_s^{i,q}} \, ds, \\ V_t^{i,q} &= x^{i,q} + \int_0^t \frac{2}{V_s^{i,q} - W_s} \, ds. \end{aligned} $$

Each reciprocal gap is required to be absolutely integrable on every compact time interval before the lifetime, and each collision set $\{t : W_t = V_t^{i,q}\}$ is required to have Lebesgue measure zero. The values assigned to the integrands on these null sets do not affect the integrals. Filtrations are completed and made right-continuous.

The continuation threshold is the infimum of times $t$ such that, for at least one side $q$,

$$ \sum_{i : V_t^{i,q} = W_t} \rho^{i,q} \le -2. $$

An empty sum is zero, so it does not trigger the threshold. We construct the evolution only before this time; endpoint statements, when used, refer to continuous limits from below. Coincident force points on one side are treated as one group whose weight is the sum of their weights.

Every evolution in Definition 2.1 has the following properties, which will also be useful in characterizing geometric paths:

<!-- page 21 -->

1. For a stopping time $\tau$ at which the driving point is separated from all force points, the process up to its next collision satisfies the SDE and is the unique strong solution driven by the increments of $B$ from its state at $\tau$. This follows from local Lipschitz continuity of the coefficients away from collisions.

2. The collision sets have Lebesgue measure zero; the side ordering is preserved throughout the evolution.

3. The force-point motions satisfy the displayed integral equations, with no additional finite-variation terms supported on collision times.

Theorem 2.2 below gives existence and uniqueness in law for this definition. Theorem 2.4 characterizes geometric Loewner paths by proving these integrated equations directly. We use $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ also for the corresponding Loewner evolution and, once its continuity has been proved, its trace.

<a id="thm-2.2"></a>
**Theorem 2.2.** Given the vector $\underline{\rho}$ and the initial values $V_0^{i,q}$, Definition 2.1 uniquely determines a joint law for $W_t$, $B_t$, and the $V_t^{i,q}$ — each defined for all $t$ up to the continuation threshold. Under this law, the values $W_t$, $B_t$, and $V_t^{i,q}$ taken together are a continuous multidimensional Markovian process indexed by $t$.

<a id="thm-2.2-proof"></a>
*Proof.* When there is only a single force point, Theorem 2.2 follows from standard facts about Bessel processes (see Chapter XI of [RY99]; recall also (2.3)) and the definition coincides with the standard definition of $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$.

If there are multiple force points but all of the $V_0^{i,q}$ are non-zero except for one (without loss of generality, we may suppose that only $V_0^{1,R}$ is possibly zero) then one can obtain existence of a process with the properties above, defined up until the first time that one of the *other* force points collides with $W_t$, using a Girsanov transformation (see the discussion of Girsanov's Theorem, e.g., in [KS91, RY99]) applied to the standard one-force-point $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\rho)$ that one would obtain if $V_0^{1,R}$ were the only force point. Girsanov's theorem applies because the remaining force points introduce a smooth drift to the Brownian motion, and the new process obtained is absolutely continuous with respect to the one-force-point process (as long as one stops at a bounded stopping time that occurs before $W_t$ gets within some fixed constant distance of one of the other force points).

One can also reverse this procedure (starting with a process defined for multiple force points and applying Girsanov's theorem to produce the process corresponding to one force point). If there were multiple possibilities for the joint laws of the $W_t$, $B_t$, and $V_t^{i,q}$ in the multiple-force-point case, then this would produce multiple possibilities for the joint laws in the single-force-point case, contradicting what we have already established.

This gives us existence and uniqueness of the law up until the first time that one of the *other* force points (besides $V_0^{1,R}$) hits $W_t$. When this happens, one can use this other

<!-- page 22 -->

force point in place of $V_0^{1,R}$ (or if this other point is on the right, it will have merged with $V_0^{1,R}$ and one can subsequently treat the two force points together) and continue until a force point other than this new one is hit. Iterating this process uniquely defines the law all the way up to the continuation threshold. Here is the nonaccumulation argument for this iteration. Amalgamate coincident marks on the same side by adding their weights. There are only finitely many same-side mergers. After these mergers have stopped, an infinite sequence of changes of active mark would have to alternate between the two sides. At the first such change fix a time $t_0$ at which the nearest left and right marks are separated, and write $L = V_{t_0}^{1,L} < R = V_{t_0}^{1,R}$ and $d = R - L > 0$. Monotonicity keeps all left marks at most $L$ and all right marks at least $R$ thereafter. Every later left-to-right or right-to-left passage crosses the fixed interval $[L + d/3, R - d/3]$, of length $d/3$. While the driver is in this interval, every force-point gap has absolute value at least $d/3$, so its total drift is bounded by $C = 3d^{-1} \sum_{i,q} |\rho^{i,q}|$; amalgamation cannot increase this bound. On a fixed finite time interval $[0, T]$, Brownian uniform continuity supplies a random $\delta > 0$ such that $\sqrt{\kappa}|B_v - B_u| + C|v - u| < d/3$ whenever $0 \le u \le v \le T$ and $v - u < \delta$. Applied between the last entrance at one endpoint and the first subsequent arrival at the other, the integrated driving equation shows that each such crossing takes at least $\delta$ units of time. Infinitely many alternating passages therefore cannot occur by time $T$. The Brownian motion here is the driving Brownian motion used to concatenate the local solutions; equivalently it can be extended independently after a putative lifetime. This argument does not presuppose continuity of $W$ at that lifetime. If the two nearest marks start together, use the entrance construction below until a positive time before the first collision with another mark; their separation is then strictly positive and the same argument applies. Thus the local constructions patch without a finite accumulation of changes of active mark.

We now construct the process when the only force points are at $0^-$ and $0^+$. Write $\rho_L, \rho_R > -2$ for their weights and put $a = \rho_L + 2$ and $b = \rho_R + 2$. (If either weight is at most $-2$, the continuation threshold is already zero.) We use a Jacobi diffusion to make the entrance from the common starting point explicit.

Let $U$ be the diffusion on $[0,1]$ satisfying

$$ dU_u = \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2} U_u (1 - U_u)} \, d\beta_u + \frac{1}{2}\{a(1 - U_u) - bU_u\} \, du. $$

The standard one-dimensional existence, pathwise-uniqueness, and comparison theorems apply: the drift is Lipschitz, the diffusion coefficient is $1/2$-Hölder, and the drift points into $[0,1]$ at both endpoints. These are ordinary Itô solutions, including when an endpoint is accessible; no extra local-time term is added. The invariant probability law is

$$ \pi(du) = \frac{u^{2a/\kappa - 1}(1 - u)^{2b/\kappa - 1}}{\mathrm{B}(2a/\kappa, 2b/\kappa)} \, du, \qquad 0 < u < 1. $$

This follows either from the one-dimensional speed measure or by writing the generator in divergence form with this density. There is a unique invariant law. Indeed, comparison

<!-- page 23 -->

couples solutions from $v \le w$ using the same Brownian motion so that $U_u^v \le U_u^w$, and the stochastic integral has mean zero since its integrand is bounded. Consequently

$$ \mathbf{E}[U_u^w - U_u^v] = e^{-(a+b)u/2}(w - v), \qquad u \ge 0. $$

Coupling also with an initial point of law $\pi$ gives uniform convergence of the transition probabilities to $\pi$ in the Wasserstein distance with cost $|v - w|$. In particular the stationary diffusion is ergodic.

Take a two-sided stationary version $(U_u)_{u \in \mathbf{R}}$ and define

$$ \begin{aligned} t(u) &= \frac{1}{2} \int_{-\infty}^{u} e^{2v} U_v (1 - U_v) \, dv, \\ L^-(u) &= -\int_{-\infty}^{u} e^{v}(1 - U_v) \, dv, \qquad L^+(u) = \int_{-\infty}^{u} e^{v} U_v \, dv, \\ w(u) &= L^-(u) + e^u U_u. \end{aligned} $$

The integrals are finite for every finite $u$, and $L^+(u) - L^-(u) = e^u$. Since $\pi$ has no endpoint atoms, the set of $u$ with $U_u \in \{0, 1\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero almost surely, by stationarity and Fubini. Thus $t$ is continuous and strictly increasing, with $t(u) \downarrow 0$ as $u \to -\infty$. Moreover $t(u) \uparrow \infty$ as $u \to \infty$: by ergodicity the integral of the nonnegative stationary process $U_v(1 - U_v)$ over $[0, u]$ tends to infinity, since its mean is strictly positive, and $e^{2v} \ge 1$ there.

Let $u(t)$ denote the inverse clock, and set

$$ V_t^L = L^-(u(t)), \qquad V_t^R = L^+(u(t)), \qquad W_t = w(u(t)), \qquad t > 0, $$

with all three processes equal to zero at time zero. The bounds $|L^-(u)|, |L^+(u)|, |w(u)| \le 2e^u$ give continuity at zero. Itô's formula yields, in the $u$ clock,

$$ dw(u) = e^u \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2} U_u (1 - U_u)} \, d\beta_u + \frac{e^u}{2}\{\rho_L (1 - U_u) - \rho_R U_u\} \, du. $$

The stochastic integral from $-\infty$ exists in $L^2$ on each interval ending at a finite $u$; its quadratic variation is $\kappa t(u)$. After the inverse time change, Lévy's characterization therefore gives a standard Brownian motion $B$ such that

$$ \begin{aligned} dW_t &= \sqrt{\kappa} \, dB_t + \left( \frac{\rho_L}{W_t - V_t^L} + \frac{\rho_R}{W_t - V_t^R} \right) dt, \\ dV_t^L &= \frac{2}{V_t^L - W_t} \, dt, \qquad dV_t^R = \frac{2}{V_t^R - W_t} \, dt. \end{aligned} $$

All reciprocal-gap integrals are finite on compact time intervals. For example,

$$ \int_0^{t(u)} \frac{ds}{W_s - V_s^L} = \frac{1}{2} \int_{-\infty}^{u} e^v (1 - U_v) \, dv < \infty, $$

<!-- page 24 -->

and the right-gap formula is analogous. The collision sets have zero Lebesgue measure in the $t$ clock. Thus this construction satisfies all three conditions of Definition 2.1.

For uniqueness, take any solution of the integrated equations from the same initial configuration and let $\ell_t = V_t^R - V_t^L$ and $X_t = (W_t - V_t^L)/\ell_t$ for $t > 0$. Monotonicity and the integral equations give $\ell_t > 0$ for $t > 0$, and

$$ \frac{d\ell_t}{dt} = \frac{2}{\ell_t X_t (1 - X_t)}, \qquad \frac{d(\ell_t^2)}{dt} = \frac{4}{X_t (1 - X_t)} \ge 16 $$

almost everywhere. The change of clock $u = \log \ell_t$ transforms $X$ into precisely the Jacobi diffusion above. Its time parameter runs from $-\infty$ to $\infty$. Here is a justification which does not presuppose the general nonexplosion lemma below. If the physical lifetime is infinite, $\ell_t^2 \ge 16t$ gives the assertion. If a maximal physical lifetime were finite and $\ell$ had a finite limit, monotonicity and $V^L \le 0 \le V^R$ would give finite limits for both force coordinates. The integrated driving identity then gives a finite limit for $W$. Since the limiting separation is positive, at most one force group can collide there; its weight is greater than $-2$. The noncoincident one-collision charts already constructed therefore continue the evolution, contradicting maximality. Thus a finite physical lifetime could only have $\ell \to \infty$, which again makes the logarithmic clock tend to infinity. Its law must be stationary: for fixed $u$, start the transition kernel at time $u - r$ and let $r \to \infty$; the uniform Wasserstein convergence proved above forces its marginal at $u$ to be $\pi$. The Markov transition kernels then force all its finite-dimensional laws to agree with those of the two-sided stationary diffusion. Integrating the force-point equations back from $-\infty$ recovers exactly $t, L^-, L^+$, and $w$ above. This proves uniqueness in law of $(W, V^L, V^R)$, and the integrated equation determines $B$ from this triple. The same argument can be localized at any finite force-point separation; hence it also establishes uniqueness before a stopping time. The transition law from every positive time is the already constructed noncoincident law, giving the Markov property.

The extension from two force points (both at the origin) to many force points (two at the origin) is the same Girsanov argument given above. The finite-coordinate endpoint and nonexplosion details for this local construction, including the critical total weight $-2$, are proved in **Aux.** lemma 3 immediately below. That lemma also identifies its maximal lifetime with the continuation threshold; it does not use continuity of the trace. $\square$

<a id="aux-3"></a>
**Aux. lemma 3** (Finite terminal coordinates of the driving construction). Consider the local driving construction in the proof of Theorem 2.2, with arbitrary finitely many real weights and $\kappa > 0$, and with a positive maximal lifetime $T_*$. Continue the local construction whenever its limiting state is admissible. Then no force coordinate escapes to infinity at a finite $T_*$. All driving and force coordinates have finite continuous limits at a finite lifetime, and at least one whole group colliding with the driver there has weight at most $-2$. Thus the maximal lifetime is precisely the continuation threshold. It is a predictable stopping time. These assertions do not use continuity of the physical trace.

<!-- page 25 -->

<a id="aux-3-proof"></a>
*Proof.* The case with no force points is immediate from the Brownian driving equation. In the other cases, all statements below are first made strictly before the maximal lifetime. Each left force coordinate is nonincreasing and each right force coordinate is nondecreasing. Differences of two coordinates on the same side are nonincreasing in absolute value, because the integrated Loewner equations give

$$ \frac{d}{dt}|V_t^i - V_t^j| = -\frac{2|V_t^i - V_t^j|}{|V_t^i - W_t| \, |V_t^j - W_t|} $$

almost everywhere; collision times have zero Lebesgue measure. In particular all same-side spreads have fixed finite bounds. The integrated equations also give

<a id="id-N1"></a>
$$ W_t = \sqrt{\kappa} B_t - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,q} \rho^{i,q} (V_t^{i,q} - x^{i,q}). \tag{N1} $$

The Brownian motion can be continued independently after a putative finite maximal lifetime, so it is bounded and uniformly continuous on each fixed finite time interval.

*A predictable localization before proving nonexplosion.* This preliminary step avoids assuming the terminal coordinate limits that we are about to prove. Put

$$ A_t = \sum_{i,q} |V_t^{i,q} - x^{i,q}|. $$

This is continuous and increasing. If $A_{T_*-} < \infty$ at a finite lifetime, monotonicity gives finite limits of all $V$'s, and (N1) gives a finite limit of $W$. If instead $A_{T_*-} = \infty$, the compactified coordinates

<a id="id-N2"></a>
$$ a_t = \frac{A_t}{1 + A_t}, \qquad v_t^{i,q} = \frac{V_t^{i,q}}{(1 + A_t)^2}, \qquad w_t = \frac{W_t}{(1 + A_t)^2} \tag{N2} $$

have limits $1, 0, 0$, respectively. The last assertion follows from $|V_t^{i,q}| \le |x^{i,q}| + A_t$ and (N1). Freeze this compactified process at its terminal limit. It is a continuous adapted process on all finite times, without any assumed finite limit for an escaping unscaled coordinate.

Its first hit of the closed face $\{a = 1\}$ detects escape. The other possible finite terminal states are detected by the following countable family of closed sets. For every side $q$, index $j$ with $\sum_{i \le j} \rho^{i,q} \le -2$, and positive integers $k, m$, use

<a id="id-N3"></a>
$$ a \le 1 - 1/k, \qquad v^{j,q} = w, \qquad |v^{j+1,q} - w| \ge 1/m, \tag{N3} $$

omitting the final condition for the last index on a side. The separation condition identifies the last index of the *whole* colliding group, rather than an inadmissible partial sum inside a larger admissible group. None of these closed sets is hit before $T_*$. If a finite terminal state with finite $A$ were admissible, its reciprocal-gap integrals would

<!-- page 26 -->

also have finite limits, since they equal half the monotone coordinate motions. A local Lipschitz chart when all gaps are positive, or a one-force Bessel chart with bounded drifts from the separated groups when a group collides, would continue the solution. Both sides cannot collide at a positive time: their cross-gap is increasing and is strictly positive after the two-sided entrance. Maximality therefore implies that a finite terminal state hits one of (N3), unless it hits the escape face.

Every positive first hit of one of these closed sets is predictable. First hits of distances $1/n$, capped at increasing deterministic horizons, announce it. Since the compactified process has been frozen, each such first hit is either $T_*$ or infinity. Their countable union is exactly the graph of $T_*$ on finite times. Thus $T_*$ is predictable. Choose increasing finite stopping times $\sigma_n < T_*$ with $\sigma_n \uparrow T_*$. This step allows the stopped Ito arguments below; it has not yet ruled out the escape face.

*At most one side could escape at a finite lifetime.* If one force coordinate on a side escapes, all coordinates on that side escape, since their spreads are bounded. A left coordinate cannot tend to $-\infty$ while $W$ stays bounded below: once $V$ is below such a bound $m$, its equation gives

$$ \frac{d}{dt}(m - V_t)^2 = \frac{4(m - V_t)}{W_t - V_t} \le 4. $$

The reflected assertion holds on the right. Consequently escape of both sides would make $W$ unbounded both below and above. At a positive time before $T_*$ fix two separated nearest coordinates $\ell < r$ on the left and right. All later left coordinates remain at most $\ell$, and all right coordinates remain at least $r$. On the fixed middle third of $[\ell, r]$, all force gaps are bounded below by $(r - \ell)/3$, so the driving drift is bounded. Brownian uniform continuity then gives a positive minimum duration for each full crossing of this middle third, exactly as in the nonaccumulation argument in the driving construction. An unbounded driver in both directions before a finite time would make infinitely many such crossings. This is impossible.

It remains to rule out escape of just one side. We give the left-side argument. Let $V^{\mathrm{out}}$ be the outermost left coordinate, put

$$ s = \sum_i \rho^{i,L}, \qquad Q = W - V^{\mathrm{out}}, \qquad \Delta_i = V^{i,L} - V^{\mathrm{out}}. $$

Then $0 \le \Delta_i \le Q$, every $\Delta_i$ is decreasing, and (N1) becomes

<a id="id-N4"></a>
$$ Q_t = \sqrt{\kappa} B_t - (1 + s/2) V_t^{\mathrm{out}} - \frac{1}{2} \sum_i \rho^{i,L} \Delta_i(t) - \frac{1}{2} \sum_i \rho^{i,R} V_t^{i,R} + C_0. \tag{N4} $$

On a putative finite left escape the right coordinates have finite limits, by the preceding paragraph. All terms in (N4) except the displayed multiple of $V^{\mathrm{out}}$ are therefore bounded. If $s < -2$, (N4) makes $Q$ tend to $-\infty$, contradicting $Q \ge 0$. If $s > -2$, it

<!-- page 27 -->

makes $Q$ tend to $+\infty$, so eventually $|dV^{\mathrm{out}}/dt| = 2/Q \le 2$; a finite-time escape is again impossible. Only $s = -2$ requires an additional estimate.

*The critical side total $s = -2$.* In this case $Q > 0$ before $T_*$, since $Q = 0$ would mean that the whole left group has collided and its total weight is already $-2$. From (N4),

<a id="id-N5"></a>
$$ dQ_t = \sqrt{\kappa} \, dB_t - \frac{1}{2} \sum_i \rho^{i,L} \, d\Delta_i(t) + b_t \, dt, \qquad b_t = -\sum_i \frac{\rho^{i,R}}{V_t^{i,R} - W_t}. \tag{N5} $$

An absent right side means $b = 0$. On a putative finite left escape, (N4) makes $Q$ bounded, while $W = V^{\mathrm{out}} + Q \to -\infty$. Thus $b$ is bounded on a sufficiently late interval. We prove that on every such bounded-$Q$, bounded-$b$ interval,

<a id="id-N6"></a>
$$ \int \frac{dt}{Q_t} < \infty \tag{N6} $$

up to its finite endpoint. This contradicts $V_t^{\mathrm{out}} = V_q^{\mathrm{out}} - \int_q^t 2/Q_u \, du$.

Here are the localization and integrability details. Start at a deterministic rational time $q$ on $\{q < T_*\}$, and condition on its history, retaining the $\mathcal{F}_q$-measurable event $\{q < T_*\}$. On that event $\sigma_n \vee q < T_*$. Fix $M \ge 2$, $C, H < \infty$, and work on $\{Q_q < M\}$, and stop on reaching $Q \ge M$, $|b| \ge C$, or time $q + H$. In addition stop at $Q \le \varepsilon$ and at $\sigma_n \vee q$. On the resulting bounded stopped interval apply Ito's formula to $F(x) = x \log x$, with $F(0) = 0$. Its stochastic integral has mean zero, since $F'$ is bounded on $[\varepsilon, M]$ and the time interval is bounded.

The spread terms have a bound independent of $n$ and $\varepsilon$. Indeed, when $\Delta_i > 0$ and $Q \le M$,

$$ |F'(Q)| = |1 + \log Q| \le 1 + \log M + (-\log \Delta_i)_+. $$

Therefore, if $w_i$ bounds the initial spread,

<a id="id-N7"></a>
$$ \int |F'(Q_t)| \, (-d\Delta_i(t)) \le (1 + \log M) w_i + \int_0^{w_i} (-\log x)_+ \, dx < \infty. \tag{N7} $$

A decreasing continuous spread stays zero once it reaches zero, so its variation measure has no contribution there. There is no assumption on the signs of the individual weights or their earlier partial sums in (N7).

Since $F''(x) = 1/x$, the second-order Ito term is $(\kappa/2) \int dt/Q_t$. For the bounded drift term use

$$ C|1 + \log x| \le \frac{\kappa}{4x} + C_{\kappa,C,M}, \qquad 0 < x \le M. $$

This inequality holds because $x|\log x| \to 0$ as $x \downarrow 0$. Taking conditional expectations in Ito's formula, bounding the two endpoint values of $F$ by its supremum on $[0, M]$, and using (N7) gives

<a id="id-N8"></a>
$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\kappa}{4} \mathbb{E}\left[ \int \frac{dt}{Q_t} \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_q \right] &\le 2 \sup_{0 \le x \le M} |x \log x| + C_{\kappa,C,M} H \\ &\quad + \frac{1}{2} \sum_i |\rho^{i,L}| \left( (1 + \log M) w_i + \int_0^{w_i} (-\log x)_+ \, dx \right). \end{aligned} \tag{N8} $$

<!-- page 28 -->

The bound is uniform in both localizations. First send $\varepsilon \downarrow 0$ at fixed $n$, and then $n \to \infty$. Monotone convergence proves (N6) up to the stated upper, drift, time, or lifetime stops. A putative finite critical escape has bounded $Q$ and eventually bounded $b$. It is covered by one of countably many rational starting times $q$ and integer choices of $M, C, H$ on which none of the first three stops occurs before $T_*$. Equation (N6) rules out escape on each such event. This proves that the critical case also cannot explode. Reflection handles right escape.

Thus $A_{T_*-} < \infty$ at every finite maximal lifetime. Monotonicity gives finite force-coordinate limits, and (N1) gives a finite driver limit. The reciprocal-gap integrals are finite through that endpoint, since each equals half the corresponding total coordinate motion. Finally, if the limiting state were admissible, the local chart used in the predictability argument would continue it. Hence a whole colliding group has weight at most $-2$, and the maximal lifetime is exactly the continuation threshold, as claimed. $\square$

<a id="aux-4"></a>
**Aux. lemma 4** (Localized continuity of the entrance law). Fix $\kappa > 0$. Let $\rho_L^{(n)}, \rho_R^{(n)} > -2$ converge to $\rho_L, \rho_R > -2$. For the two-force-point evolutions started with both force points at $0^-, 0^+$, the joint laws of $(W, V^L, V^R, B)$ converge weakly, uniformly on every compact interval of physical time, to the law with limiting weights.

The same assertion holds locally after adding finitely many force points initially separated from zero, if their initial positions and weights converge. Here "locally" can be given the following precise meaning. Replace the reciprocal-gap drifts of these additional points by one fixed smooth bounded function that agrees with the reciprocal on gaps of absolute value at least $\delta > 0$. The resulting joint driving and force-point laws converge on every fixed finite time interval. Up to the first additional-force-point gap of size $\delta$, these laws agree with the unmodified SLE laws. Thus one may use this convergence in any experiment confined to this localization.

<a id="aux-4-proof"></a>
*Proof.* Put $a_n = \rho_L^{(n)} + 2$, $b_n = \rho_R^{(n)} + 2$, and similarly $a, b > 0$. Use the stationary Jacobi construction in the proof of Theorem 2.2. Its invariant laws $\operatorname{Beta}(2a_n/\kappa, 2b_n/\kappa)$ converge to the limiting beta law. On every bounded interval of logarithmic time the drifts are uniformly bounded and the diffusion coefficient is the same bounded continuous function. The fourth-moment estimate for stochastic integrals gives

$$ \mathbf{E}|U_v^{(n)} - U_u^{(n)}|^4 \le C(|v - u|^2 + |v - u|^4). $$

Consequently these stationary path laws are tight on $C_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbf{R}, [0,1])$. The martingale problem passes to every subsequential limit, and weak uniqueness for the Jacobi equation, with the limiting invariant initial law, identifies that limit. Therefore $U^{(n)} \Rightarrow U$ on this path space.

By the Skorokhod representation theorem we may verify continuity of the construction under almost sure uniform convergence on every bounded logarithmic-time interval.

The lower tails of its defining integrals obey the deterministic bounds

$$ \frac{1}{2} \int_{-\infty}^{-R} e^{2v} U_v^{(n)} (1 - U_v^{(n)}) \, dv \le \frac{e^{-2R}}{16}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{-R} e^v U_v^{(n)} \, dv \le e^{-R}, $$

and the same second bound holds with $1 - U_v^{(n)}$. Thus $t_n, L_n^-, L_n^+$, and $w_n$ converge uniformly on bounded logarithmic-time intervals. Almost surely the limiting clock $t$ is continuous and strictly increasing from zero to infinity. The standard inverse-function argument then gives convergence of the time-changed paths uniformly on each physical interval $[\epsilon, T]$. Continuity at physical time zero is uniform as well. Indeed, if $t \le t_n(-R)$, then the separation is at most $e^{-R}$ and

$$ |W_t^{(n)}| \vee |V_t^{L,(n)}| \vee |V_t^{R,(n)}| \le e^{-R}. $$

For each fixed $R$, $t_n(-R) \to t(-R) > 0$. This proves convergence uniformly on $[0, T]$. Finally, the integrated equations give

$$ B_t^{(n)} = \kappa^{-1/2} \left( W_t^{(n)} + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho_L^{(n)} V_t^{L,(n)} + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho_R^{(n)} V_t^{R,(n)} \right), $$

which proves joint convergence including the Brownian motion.

For the additional marks, choose a smooth bounded function $r_\delta$ with bounded derivative that equals $1/x$ for $|x| \ge \delta$. Under the two-force-point reference law, solve

$$ dY_t^{j,(n)} = 2 r_\delta (Y_t^{j,(n)} - W_t^{(n)}) \, dt, \qquad Y_0^{j,(n)} = x_j^{(n)}. $$

Solutions of these equations are continuous functions of their initial conditions and of the driving path in the uniform topology, by Gronwall's inequality. In particular, the joint paths converge. Put

$$ D_t^{(n)} = \sum_j \rho_j^{(n)} r_\delta (W_t^{(n)} - Y_t^{j,(n)}). $$

These adapted continuous processes are uniformly bounded on a fixed time interval $[0, T]$ and converge jointly with the reference paths. Change measure by

$$ Z_n = \exp\left\{ \kappa^{-1/2} \int_0^T D_s^{(n)} \, dB_s^{(n)} - \frac{1}{2\kappa} \int_0^T (D_s^{(n)})^2 \, ds \right\}. $$

Novikov's condition holds. Under the probability measure with density $Z_n$, the process

$$ \widetilde{B}_t^{(n)} = B_t^{(n)} - \kappa^{-1/2} \int_0^t D_s^{(n)} \, ds $$

is Brownian motion, and the driving equation written with $\widetilde{B}^{(n)}$ has exactly the additional drift $D^{(n)}$. The unadjusted reference process $B^{(n)}$ is not asserted to remain Brownian under the changed measure.

<!-- page 30 -->

We spell out why these changes of measure preserve convergence. Approximate each stochastic integral by its left-endpoint sums on deterministic time partitions. The uniform bound and tightness of $D^{(n)}$ in the continuous-path topology imply that the expected integrated squared errors of the step approximations tend to zero uniformly in $n$ as the mesh tends to zero. Itô's isometry gives the same conclusion for the stochastic integrals. Each fixed-partition sum is a continuous function of the joint paths $(D^{(n)}, B^{(n)})$. The approximation argument proves joint convergence of these integrals and hence of $Z_n$. Moreover, if $|D^{(n)}| \le C$ and $p > 1$, then

$$ \sup_n \mathbf{E} Z_n^p \le \exp\left\{ \frac{p(p-1)C^2 T}{2\kappa} \right\} < \infty. $$

Uniform integrability therefore lets us pass to the limit in $\mathbf{E}[Z_n F]$ for every bounded continuous path functional $F$. This proves weak convergence under the changed measures. It also proves joint convergence with the actual driving Brownian motions: the map $(B, D) \mapsto B - \kappa^{-1/2} \int_0^{\cdot} D_s \, ds$ is continuous in the uniform path topology. Inverse Girsanov and the already proved two-force-point uniqueness identify these changed laws with the unique localized equations. Before an additional gap first reaches $\delta$, the smoothing is inactive, so the unmodified SLE law is recovered there. $\square$

**Aux. lemma 5** (A localized repulsion criterion). Let $Y$ be a continuous nonnegative semimartingale, up to a possibly infinite lifetime, with quadratic variation $\kappa t$, where $\kappa > 0$. Suppose its zero set has Lebesgue measure zero and, on every compact subinterval before its lifetime,

$$ dY_t = \sqrt{\kappa} \, d\beta_t + b_t \, dt, \qquad \int |b_t| \, dt < \infty, $$

where $\beta$ is Brownian motion in the retained filtration. Fix $a > 0$ and $C \ge 0$. On a stopped interval on which $0 < Y_t < a$, suppose

<a id="id-RP1"></a>
$$ b_t \ge \frac{\kappa}{2Y_t} - C \quad \text{for almost every } t. \tag{RP1} $$

<a id="aux-5-proof"></a>
*Proof.* For $0 < y \le a$ set

$$ F(y) = \int_y^a \frac{e^{2Cs/\kappa}}{s} \, ds. $$

<a id="id-RP2"></a>
$$ F'(y) = -\frac{e^{2Cy/\kappa}}{y}, \qquad \frac{\kappa}{2} F''(y) + \left( \frac{\kappa}{2y} - C \right) F'(y) = 0. \tag{RP2} $$

Since $F' < 0$, (RP1) makes the finite-variation term in Itô's formula for $F(Y)$ nonpositive. Stop first at $\varepsilon$, at $a$, and at a bounded time horizon, and localize the remaining coefficients if necessary. The resulting nonnegative local supermartingale is a supermartingale: applying conditional Fatou to its localizations proves this statement directly. Thus, conditionally on the starting history,

<a id="id-RP3"></a>
$$ \mathbb{P}\{Y \text{ reaches } \varepsilon \text{ before } a \text{ and before the other stops}\} \le \frac{F(Y_{\mathrm{start}})}{F(\varepsilon)}. \tag{RP3} $$

### 2.3 Martingale characterization of $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\rho)$

The $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ processes are singled out by the following martingale characterization, which we will use repeatedly. A version of this result for $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa$ ($\underline{\rho} \equiv 0$) appears in [Dub09b, Section 7.2]. The argument that we present here is similar to the one in [Dub09b].

If we are given any process $W_t$ with $W_0 = 0$ we can define the Loewner evolution $g_t$. If we are also given a set of points $x^{i,L} \le 0$ and $x^{i,R} \ge 0$ then we can define processes $V_t^{i,q}$ such that if $x^{i,q}$ has not yet been absorbed by the Loewner hull $K_t$ then $V_t^{i,q} = g_t(x^{i,q})$, and otherwise $V_t^{i,L}$ (resp., $V_t^{i,R}$) is the $g_t$ image of the left (resp., right) endpoint of $\mathbf{R} \cap K_t$.

<a id="thm-2.4"></a>
**Theorem 2.4.** Suppose we are given a random continuous curve $\eta$ on $\overline{\mathbf{H}}$ from $0$ to $\infty$ whose Loewner driving function $W_t$ is almost surely continuous. Suppose that $x^{i,q}$ and $\rho^{i,q}$ values are given and that the $V_t^{i,q}$ are defined to be the images of the $x^{i,q}$ under the corresponding Loewner evolution (as described just above). Let $\mathfrak{h}_t$ be the corresponding harmonic function in the statement of Theorem 1.1. Then $W_t$ and the $V_t^{i,q}$ can be

<!-- page 35 -->

coupled with a standard Brownian motion $B_t$ to describe an $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ process (up to the continuation threshold) if and only if $\mathfrak{h}_t(z)$ evolves as a continuous local martingale in $t$ for each fixed $z \in \mathbf{H}$ until the time $z$ is absorbed by $K_t$.

The assumption that $\eta$ has a continuous Loewner driving function implies that $\eta$ is non-self-tracing and does not trace $\partial\mathbf{H}$ and that $\eta$ does not enter into the bounded components that it draws. See Proposition 6.12.

<a id="thm-2.4-proof"></a>
*Proof of Theorem 2.4.* We use the usual augmentation of the filtration of the curve. All statements below are stopped strictly before the continuation threshold, and at a bounded time when necessary. Write $j$ for the finitely many force-point indices, write $\rho_j$ for their weights, and put $f_t = g_t - W_t$. With logarithms of points in $\mathbf{H}$ having imaginary parts in $(0, \pi)$ and with the continuous branch of $\log g_t'$ initialized at zero, define

$$ \begin{aligned} H_t(z) = {} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}} \left[ i\pi \left( 1 + \sum_{j \text{ on the right}} \rho_j \right) - 2 \log f_t(z) \right] \\ & - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}} \sum_j \rho_j \log(g_t(z) - V_t^j) - \chi \log g_t'(z). \end{aligned} \tag{2.5} $$

Then $\mathfrak{h}_t(z) = \operatorname{Im} H_t(z)$. Here the boundary discontinuities of the harmonic function composed with $f_t$ occur at $V_t^j - W_t$. This identity follows by taking boundary values of the logarithms; it also fixes all normalization constants in the argument.

We first prove the converse implication. Suppose that $\mathfrak{h}_t(z)$ is a continuous local martingale for each deterministic $z$, up to its swallowing time. The geometric force-point images $V^j$ are continuous and monotone: left images are non-increasing and right images are non-decreasing. Away from $V_t^j = W_t$, they satisfy the ordinary Loewner equation. These are deterministic properties of the real boundary maps. In particular, the $V^j$ have locally finite variation even before their differential equations have been justified on collision times.

*The driving function is a semimartingale.* Fix a finite time horizon $T$ and a point $z = iY$ with $Y^2 > 4T$. The Loewner equation gives $\operatorname{Im}(g_t(z))^2 \ge Y^2 - 4t$, so this point is not swallowed before $T$. Every term on the right of (2.5) other than $\log f_t(z)$ has locally finite variation. It follows that $\arg f_t(z)$ is a continuous semimartingale. Since

$$ W_t = \operatorname{Re} g_t(z) - \operatorname{Im} g_t(z) \cot(\arg f_t(z)), $$

localization inside $(0, \pi)$ and Itô's formula show that $W$ is a continuous semimartingale on $[0, T]$. Write $W = M + A$, with $M_0 = A_0 = 0$, $M$ a continuous local martingale and $A$ continuous of locally finite variation, and write $Q = \langle M \rangle$.

*The possible singular force-point motions.* Let $Z_j = \{t : V_t^j = W_t\}$. Lemma 2.5 implies that $Z_j$ has Lebesgue measure zero. On its complement, $dV_t^j = 2(V_t^j - W_t)^{-1} dt$.

<!-- page 36 -->

Monotonicity therefore gives

$$ \int_0^T \frac{2 \, dt}{|V_t^j - W_t|} \le |V_T^j - V_0^j| < \infty, $$

where the integrand is set to zero on $Z_j$. Thus the continuous process

$$ I_t^j = V_t^j - V_0^j - \int_0^t \frac{2}{V_s^j - W_s} \, ds $$

has finite variation, its Stieltjes measure is supported on $Z_j$, and $I^j$ is non-increasing for a left force point and non-decreasing for a right force point. Put $\mu_t = \sum_j \rho_j / (W_t - V_t^j)$, with arbitrary values on the null collision set. This process is locally integrable.

*Identification of the quadratic variation and drift.* Itô's formula applied to (2.5), using $2\sqrt{\kappa}\chi = 4 - \kappa$, gives

$$ \begin{aligned} dH_t(z) = {} & \frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa} f_t(z)} \, dM_t + \frac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa} f_t(z)} (dA_t - \mu_t dt) \\ & + \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa} f_t(z)^2} (dQ_t - \kappa dt) + \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}} \sum_j \frac{\rho_j}{g_t(z) - V_t^j} \, dI_t^j. \end{aligned} $$

On the support of $dI^j$, the denominator in its last term is $f_t(z)$. Consequently, with the real signed measures

$$ dC_t = 2(dA_t - \mu_t dt) + \sum_j \rho_j \, dI_t^j, \qquad dR_t = dQ_t - \kappa dt, $$

the local-martingale hypothesis says

$$ \operatorname{Im} \frac{1}{f_t(z)} \, dC_t + \operatorname{Im} \frac{1}{f_t(z)^2} \, dR_t = 0. \tag{2.6} $$

For completeness, this identity determines both measures without assuming that a harmonic conjugate is a local martingale. Work on a localized compact time interval and dominate the variations of $C$ and $R$ by a common finite positive measure. Apply (2.6) simultaneously to a countable dense set of deterministic points, stopping each before it is swallowed. Outside one null set, the resulting identities hold for almost every time with respect to the dominating measure. Continuity in $z$ extends them to every $z$ in the unbounded component of the complement of the hull. Since $f_t$ maps this domain onto $\mathbf{H}$, the real densities $c, r$ satisfy

$$ \operatorname{Im}(c/w + r/w^2) = 0 \qquad (w \in \mathbf{H}). $$

Taking $w = i$ gives $c = 0$, and taking $w = 1 + i$ then gives $r = 0$. Hence

$$ dQ_t = \kappa dt, \qquad dA_t = \mu_t dt - \frac{1}{2} \sum_j \rho_j \, dI_t^j. \tag{2.7} $$

<!-- page 37 -->

*The singular motions vanish.* We give the argument for a left force point; the right-side argument has reversed signs. Merge coincident force points on the same side and add their weights. Geometric force points which merge remain together, and there are at most finitely many such merging times. Apart from those times and time zero, each collision is locally a collision with one distinct force-point group, separated from all other force points. Left and right groups are strictly separated at positive times. The continuous Stieltjes measures $dI^j$ have no atoms, so it suffices to work on such local time intervals.

Let $V$ be the colliding left group, let $\rho$ be its total weight, and let $dI$ be the common defect measure of its force points on this interval (choose any common additive normalization for $I$ there). Before the continuation threshold, $\rho > -2$. The non-negative semimartingale $X = W - V$ has quadratic variation $d\langle X \rangle_t = \kappa dt$ and satisfies

$$ dX_t = dM_t + b_t dt - \left( 1 + \frac{\rho}{2} \right) dI_t, $$

where $b$ is locally integrable; the other force points have no singular motion on the interval under consideration. The integrability already proved implies

$$ \frac{1}{\epsilon} \int_s^t \mathbf{1}_{\{0 < X_u < \epsilon\}} \, d\langle X \rangle_u \le \kappa \int_s^t \frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{0 < X_u < \epsilon\}}}{X_u} \, du \longrightarrow 0. $$

The occupation-density formula therefore gives zero right local time of $X$ at zero on this interval. Tanaka's formula for the non-negative process $X$ yields $\int_s^t \mathbf{1}_{\{X_u = 0\}} \, dX_u = 0$. The martingale term in this integral is zero because its quadratic variation is $\kappa \int_s^t \mathbf{1}_{\{X_u = 0\}} du = 0$, and the absolutely continuous term is zero for the same reason. Since $dI$ is supported on $\{X = 0\}$,

$$ 0 = -\left( 1 + \frac{\rho}{2} \right) (I_t - I_s). $$

Thus $I$ is constant. For a right group, use $X = V - W$ and obtain the same conclusion. A countable cover of the collision times by the local intervals just used, together with the absence of atoms at merging times, proves $I^j \equiv 0$ for every force point.

It now follows from (2.7) that

$$ W_t = \sqrt{\kappa} B_t + \int_0^t \sum_j \frac{\rho_j}{W_s - V_s^j} \, ds, \qquad V_t^j = V_0^j + \int_0^t \frac{2}{V_s^j - W_s} \, ds, $$

where $B = M/\sqrt{\kappa}$ is a Brownian motion by Lévy's characterization (up to the stopping time under consideration, and extended independently thereafter if necessary). Away from collisions the coefficients are locally Lipschitz, so the strong-solution clause in Definition 2.1 also holds. This proves the required $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ characterization.

<!-- page 38 -->

Conversely, for a process satisfying the integrated SLE equations, $dI^j = 0$, $dA = \mu dt$, and $dQ = \kappa dt$. The preceding calculation reduces to

$$ dH_t(z) = \frac{2}{f_t(z)} \, dB_t, \qquad d\mathfrak{h}_t(z) = \operatorname{Im} \frac{2}{f_t(z)} \, dB_t. $$

The latter is a continuous local martingale before $z$ is swallowed, as claimed. $\square$

<a id="lem-2.5"></a>
**Lemma 2.5.** Suppose that $\eta$ is a continuous (non-random) curve on $\overline{\mathbf{H}}$ from $0$ to $\infty$ with a continuous Loewner driving function $W_t$. Then the set $\{t : \eta(t) \in \mathbf{R}\}$ has Lebesgue measure zero.

<a id="lem-2.5-proof"></a>
*Proof.* We use half-plane capacity normalized so that $\operatorname{hcap}(K_t) = 2t$. For compact sets attached to $\mathbf{R}$, take their filled hulls when writing capacity. The standard subadditivity and composition rules for half-plane capacity imply

$$ \operatorname{hcap}(A \cup B \cup C) - \operatorname{hcap}(A \cup B) \le \operatorname{hcap}(A \cup C) - \operatorname{hcap}(A). $$

Indeed, map out $A$ and apply subadditivity to the images of $B \setminus A$ and $C \setminus A$. Subadditivity itself follows from the Brownian representation of capacity, or from the maximum principle applied to the sum of the two corresponding harmonic functions.

Fix $R > 0$, choose $R' > R$, and let

$$ S_\delta = \{x + iy : -R' < x < R', \ 0 \le y < \delta\}, $$

which is relatively open in $\overline{\mathbf{H}}$. Its inverse image under $\eta$ is relatively open in $[0, \infty)$, hence a countable union of disjoint intervals. Retain those intervals on which the curve hits $\mathbf{R}$. For every retained interval $(s_j, t_j)$ (with the evident convention at time zero), the compact path segment $C_j = \eta([s_j, t_j])$ lies in $\overline{S}_\delta$ and is attached to $\mathbf{R}$. Its endpoints are finite: a curve remaining in this bounded rectangle for arbitrarily long capacity time would contradict the finiteness of the capacity of its union with the earlier hull.

Take any finite collection of retained intervals, ordered by time. For one such interval let $A$ be the union of the previously selected segments and let $B = \eta([0, s_j])$. Since $A \subseteq B$, the displayed capacity inequality gives

$$ 2(t_j - s_j) \le \operatorname{hcap}(A \cup C_j) - \operatorname{hcap}(A). $$

Summing telescopes. The final union is contained in $\overline{S}_\delta$, so

$$ 2 \sum_j (t_j - s_j) \le \operatorname{hcap}(\overline{S}_\delta). $$

Taking the supremum over finite subcollections proves the same bound for all the retained intervals. Every time with $\eta(t) \in [-R, R]$ belongs to a retained interval. Finally, $\operatorname{hcap}(\overline{S}_\delta) \to 0$ as $\delta \downarrow 0$ (for example, by continuity of half-plane capacity for this decreasing family of rectangular hulls). Hence the set of these times has Lebesgue measure zero. Taking $R$ through the positive integers proves the lemma. $\square$

<!-- page 39 -->

**Geometric force endpoints.** Consider an integrated $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ driving evolution before its continuation threshold. Let $L_t, R_t$ be the images of the left and right extreme boundary prime ends of its filled hull. Equivalently, they are the left and right endpoints of the interval omitted by the real boundary map on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \overline{K_t}$; set $L_0 = R_0 = 0$. This definition does not evaluate $g_t$ on the interior of $K_t$ and does not assume trace continuity. The deterministic real Loewner equations imply that $L$ is non-increasing and $R$ is non-decreasing, and that $L_t \le W_t \le R_t$. Away from an equality with $W$ each endpoint satisfies the ordinary Loewner equation. These facts also follow by approaching an extreme prime end through unswallowed real points. For completeness, their continuity follows from the deterministic bound

$$ R_s \le R_t \le \max\{R_s, \sup_{s \le u \le t} W_u\} + 2\sqrt{t - s}, \qquad s \le t, $$

and its reflection for $L$. To prove the upper bound, put $M = \max\{R_s, \sup_{s \le u \le t} W_u\}$ and start a real characteristic at $M + \epsilon$ at time $s$. It remains to the right of the driver and hence is not swallowed. If its image is $Z_u$, then $\frac{d}{du}(Z_u - M)^2 = 4(Z_u - M)/(Z_u - W_u) \le 4$. It lies above the rightmost omitted endpoint; let $\epsilon \downarrow 0$ to obtain the bound. As $W$ is continuous and $R_s \ge W_s$, this gives both one-sided continuity limits for $R$; reflection treats $L$. It also makes the endpoints finite on compact time intervals.

We justify that no singular motion is hidden at an equality with the driver. Write

$$ W_t = \sqrt{\kappa} B_t + \int_0^t \mu_s \, ds, \qquad \mu_s = \sum_{i,q} \frac{\rho^{i,q}}{W_s - V_s^{i,q}}, $$

where $\mu$ is locally integrable by the integrated definition. For the right endpoint put $X = R - W \ge 0$. Monotonicity and the ordinary equation on $\{X > 0\}$ give

$$ \int_0^t \frac{2\mathbf{1}_{\{X_s > 0\}}}{X_s} \, ds \le R_t - R_0 < \infty, \qquad dR_t = \frac{2\mathbf{1}_{\{X_t > 0\}}}{X_t} \, dt + dJ_t, $$

where $J$ is continuous and non-decreasing and its Stieltjes measure is supported on $\{X = 0\}$. The process $X$ is a continuous semimartingale with $d\langle X \rangle_t = \kappa \, dt$. The occupation-density formula first implies that $\{X = 0\}$ has zero Lebesgue measure. Moreover

$$ \frac{1}{\epsilon} \int_0^t \mathbf{1}_{\{0 < X_s < \epsilon\}} \, d\langle X \rangle_s \le \kappa \int_0^t \frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{0 < X_s < \epsilon\}}}{X_s} \, ds \longrightarrow 0. $$

Thus the right local time of $X$ at zero is zero. Tanaka's formula for the non-negative process $X$ therefore gives $\int_0^t \mathbf{1}_{\{X_s = 0\}} \, dX_s = 0$. The Brownian integral in this identity is zero by its quadratic variation, and the absolutely continuous terms are zero because the collision set has zero Lebesgue measure. The remaining term is $J_t - J_0$. Consequently $J$ is constant, and

$$ R_t = R_0 + \int_0^t \frac{2}{R_s - W_s} \, ds. $$

<!-- page 40 -->

Reflection proves $L_t = L_0 + \int_0^t 2/(L_s - W_s) \, ds$. As elsewhere, the integrands are set arbitrarily on their null collision sets.

Finally, once $R$ and a right force coordinate $V$ agree, they remain equal. Their difference is absolutely continuous, and away from the null collision sets it satisfies

$$ d(R - V) = -\frac{2(R - V)}{(R - W)(V - W)} \, dt. $$

Hence $|R - V|$ is non-increasing after a time at which it is zero; the chain rule for absolutely continuous functions justifies this assertion without dividing by a gap at a collision. The left-side statement is identical. This proves the endpoint identities for all $\kappa > 0$ directly from the integrated equations.

Although we will not use it in this paper, we remark (and sketch a proof) that it is possible to give an alternate martingale characterization in which one only requires $\mathfrak{h}_t(z)$ to be a local martingale for a single point $z$, but one requires a particular form for its quadratic variation:

<a id="thm-2.6"></a>
**Theorem 2.6.** Use the hypotheses, geometric force-point images, harmonic function $\mathfrak{h}_t$, and augmented curve filtration of Theorem 2.4. Suppose that for one deterministic $z \in \mathbf{H}$, $\mathfrak{h}_t(z)$ is a continuous local martingale in this filtration and

$$ \langle \mathfrak{h}(z) \rangle_t = \log \frac{\operatorname{crad}(\mathbf{H}, z)}{\operatorname{crad}(\mathbf{H} \setminus K_t, z)}. $$

Here the conformal radius is taken in the unbounded component containing $z$, and the identity is required before $z$ is swallowed and before the continuation threshold. Then the driving process is $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ up to the minimum of these two times. Equivalently, $\mathfrak{h}_t(z) - \mathfrak{h}_0(z)$ has the standard Brownian martingale parameterization given by this conformal-radius decrease; no factor $\sqrt{\kappa}$ occurs in that parameterization.

<a id="thm-2.6-proof"></a>
*Proof.* The semimartingale argument in the proof of Theorem 2.4 applies using this one point $z$, after localization before it is swallowed. In its notation,

$$ d\langle \mathfrak{h}(z) \rangle_t = \frac{4}{\kappa} \left( \operatorname{Im} \frac{1}{f_t(z)} \right)^2 dQ_t. $$

The ordinary Loewner equation, independently of the choice of driving function, gives

$$ -d \log \operatorname{crad}(\mathbf{H} \setminus K_t, z) = 4 \left( \operatorname{Im} \frac{1}{f_t(z)} \right)^2 dt. $$

Because $f_t(z) \in \mathbf{H}$, its reciprocal has nonzero imaginary part. Thus the prescribed bracket implies $dQ_t = \kappa dt$. Equation (2.6), now for this single point, then implies $dC_t = 0$. The occupation-density and Tanaka argument in the proof of Theorem 2.4 eliminates every singular force-point motion $I^j$, giving the integrated SLE equations. All statements are localized before the two stopping times specified in the theorem. $\square$

## 3 The Gaussian free field

### 3.1 Construction and basic properties

We will now describe the construction of the two-dimensional GFF as well as some properties that will be important for us later. The reader is referred to [She07] for a more detailed introduction. Let $D \subseteq \mathbf{C}$ be open with harmonically non-trivial boundary. By this, we mean that a Brownian motion started at $z \in D$ almost surely hits $\partial D$. Let $C_0^\infty(D)$ denote the set of $C^\infty$ functions compactly supported in $D$.

We let $H(D)$ be the Hilbert-space closure of $C_0^\infty(D)$ equipped with the *Dirichlet inner product*:

$$ (f, g)_\nabla = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_D \nabla f(x) \cdot \nabla g(x) dx \quad \text{for} \quad f, g \in C_0^\infty(D). $$

The GFF $h$ on $D$ can be expressed as a random linear combination of a $(\cdot,\cdot)_\nabla$-orthonormal basis $(\phi_n)$ of $H(D)$

$$ h = \sum_n \alpha_n \phi_n, \quad (\alpha_n) \quad \text{i.i.d.} \quad N(0,1). $$

Although this expansion of $h$ does not converge in $H(D)$, it does converge almost surely in the space of distributions or (when $D$ is bounded) in the fractional Sobolev space $H^{-\epsilon}(D)$ for each $\epsilon > 0$ (see [She07, Proposition 2.7] and the discussion thereafter). Let $(\cdot,\cdot)$ denote the standard $L^2(D)$ inner product. If $f, g \in C_0^\infty(D)$ then an integration by parts gives $(f, g)_\nabla = -(2\pi)^{-1}(f, \Delta g)$. Using this, we define

$$ (h, f)_\nabla = -\frac{1}{2\pi}(h, \Delta f) \quad \text{for} \quad f \in C_0^\infty(D). \tag{3.1} $$

Observe that $(h, f)_\nabla$ is a Gaussian random variable with mean zero and variance $(f, f)_\nabla$. Hence $h$ induces a map $C_0^\infty(D) \to \mathcal{G}$, $\mathcal{G}$ a Gaussian Hilbert space, that preserves the Dirichlet inner product. This map extends uniquely to $H(D)$ and allows us to make sense of $(h, f)_\nabla$ for all $f \in H(D)$ and, moreover,

$$ \operatorname{Cov}((h, f)_\nabla, (h, g)_\nabla) = (f, g)_\nabla \quad \text{for all} \quad f, g \in H(D). \tag{3.2} $$

More generally, if $D \subseteq \mathbf{C}$ is not necessarily connected, then the GFF on $D$ is given by taking $h$ to be independently a GFF on each of the components of $D$.

Suppose that $U \subseteq D$ with $U \ne D$ is open. There is a natural inclusion $\iota$ of $H(U)$ into $H(D)$ where

$$ \iota(f)(x) = \begin{cases} f(x) & \text{if} \quad x \in U, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} $$

<!-- page 42 -->

We define the following $\sigma$-algebras. For $U \subseteq D$ open, we let $\mathcal{F}_U^h$ be the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the restriction $h|_U$ of $h$ to $U$. In other words, $\mathcal{F}_U^h$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $(h, f)$ for $f \in C_0^\infty(U)$. For every closed set $K \subseteq D$, we let $\mathcal{F}_{K+}^h$ be the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the projection of $h$ onto $H^\perp(D \setminus K)$. Throughout this article, we often consider conditional expectations where we condition on $h|_V$ for $V \subseteq D$ and by this we mean that we consider the conditional expectation given $\mathcal{F}_V^h$ (resp. $\mathcal{F}_{V+}^h$) if $V$ is open (resp. closed) in $D$.

If $f \in C_0^\infty(U)$ and $g \in C_0^\infty(D)$, then as $(f, g)_\nabla = -(2\pi)^{-1}(f, \Delta g)$ it is easy to see that $H(D)$ admits the $(\cdot,\cdot)_\nabla$-orthogonal decomposition $H(U) \oplus H^\perp(U)$ where $H^\perp(U)$ is the set of functions in $H(D)$ harmonic on $U$. Thus we can write

$$ h = h_U + h_{U^c} = \sum_n \alpha_n^U \phi_n^U + \sum_n \alpha_n^{U^c} \phi_n^{U^c} $$

where $(\alpha_n^U), (\alpha_n^{U^c})$ are independent i.i.d. sequences of standard normal random variables and $(\phi_n^U), (\phi_n^{U^c})$ are orthonormal bases of $H(U)$ and $H^\perp(U)$, respectively. Observe that $h_U$ is a GFF on $U$. The distribution $h_{U^c}$ is a random distribution that agrees with $h$ on $U^c$. The restriction of $h_{U^c}$ to $U$ can be interpreted as the "harmonic extension" of $h|_{\partial U}$ to $U$. Note that $h_U$ and $h_{U^c}$ are independent. We arrive at the following proposition:

<a id="prop-3.1"></a>
**Proposition 3.1** (Markov Property). We can write $h = h_U + h_{U^c}$ where $h_U, h_{U^c}$ are independent distributions on $D$, $h_U$ has the law of a zero boundary GFF on $U$ and is zero on $D \setminus U$, and $h_{U^c}$ is harmonic on $U$. That is, the conditional law of $h|_U$ given $h|_{D \setminus U}$ is that of the GFF on $U$ plus the harmonic extension (in the sense described just above) of $h|_{\partial U}$ to $U$.

We emphasize that $h|_U$ in the statement of Proposition 3.1 refers to the *restriction* of $h$ to $U$ while $h_U$ above refers to the projection of $h$ to $H(U)$. We remark that the orthogonality of $H(U)$ and the set of functions in $H(D)$ which are harmonic on $U$ is also proved in [She07, Theorem 2.17] and it is explained thereafter how this is related to the Markov property of the GFF. The proposition allows us to make sense of the GFF with non-zero boundary conditions: if $f : \partial D \to \mathbf{R}$ is any function that is $L^1$ with respect to harmonic measure on $\partial D$ viewed from some point in each connected component of $D$ (hence every point in that component), and $F$ is its harmonic extension from $\partial D$ to $D$, then the law of the GFF on $D$ with boundary condition $f$ is given by the law of $F + h$ where $h$ is a zero boundary GFF on $D$.

Using (3.1) and (3.2), we can derive the covariance function for $(h, f), (h, g)$ for $f, g \in C_0^\infty(D)$. Namely, we have that

$$ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}((h, f), (h, g)) &= (2\pi)^2 \operatorname{Cov}((h, \Delta^{-1} f)_\nabla, (h, \Delta^{-1} g)_\nabla) = (2\pi)^2 (\Delta^{-1} f, \Delta^{-1} g)_\nabla \\ &= -2\pi (f, \Delta^{-1} g) = \iint f(x) G(x, y) g(y) \, dx dy. \end{aligned} \tag{3.3} $$

<!-- page 43 -->

where $G$ is the Green's function for $\Delta$ on $D$ with Dirichlet boundary conditions. That is, $G$ solves $\Delta G(x, y) = -2\pi \delta_x(y)$ (in the distributional sense) where $\delta_x$ denotes the Dirac mass at $x$ and $G(x, y) = 0$ if $x$ or $y$ is in $\partial D$. We note that the Green's function is monotone in $D$ in the sense that if $D \subseteq \widetilde{D}$ and $G$ (resp. $\widetilde{G}$) is the Green's function on $D$ (resp. $\widetilde{D}$) then $G(x, y) \le \widetilde{G}(x, y)$ for all $x, y \in D$. This can be seen because $\widetilde{G} - G$ is harmonic in $D$ (viewed as a function of one of the variables with the other fixed) and has non-negative boundary conditions on $\partial D$. We also note that the law of $h$ is determined by $(h, f)$ as $f$ ranges over $C_0^\infty(D)$.

We are now going to show that $\mathcal{F}_{K+}^h$ is given by the intersection of $\mathcal{F}_U^h$ over all $U \subseteq D$ open containing $K$.

<a id="prop-3.2"></a>
**Proposition 3.2.** For any deterministic, closed set $K \subseteq D$ we have that $\mathcal{F}_{K+}^h = \cap_{U \supseteq K} \mathcal{F}_U^h$ where the intersection is over all open sets $U \subseteq D$ containing $K$.

<a id="prop-3.2-proof"></a>
*Proof.* We work with completed sigma-algebras. Let $\mathcal{H} = H(D)$ and write $X(v) = (h, v)_\nabla$ for its isonormal Gaussian process. For a test function $\phi \in C_0^\infty(D)$, let $R\phi \in \mathcal{H}$ be the Dirichlet Riesz representer, characterized by

$$ (R\phi, v)_\nabla = \int_D \phi v, \qquad v \in \mathcal{H}. $$

Thus $(h, \phi) = X(R\phi)$. The existence of these representers is the usual Greenian-domain assumption under which the zero-boundary GFF in this section is defined. For an open set $U \subseteq D$, put

$$ S(U) = \overline{\operatorname{span}}\{R\phi : \phi \in C_0^\infty(U)\} \subseteq \mathcal{H}. $$

Then $\mathcal{F}_U^h = \sigma(X(v) : v \in S(U))$. Its orthogonal complement is

$$ S(U)^\perp = \{v \in H(D) : v = 0 \text{ distributionally on } U\}. $$

If $U$ contains $K$ and $v \in S(U)^\perp$, then $v \in H(D \setminus K)$. Here $H(D \setminus K)$ is the Dirichlet closure of $C_0^\infty(D \setminus K)$ in $H(D)$. We use the standard ideal property of this Dirichlet space: this closure consists of the functions in $H(D)$ whose quasi-continuous representatives vanish quasi-everywhere on $K$, where "quasi-everywhere" means outside a set of Dirichlet capacity zero. A Sobolev function that vanishes on the open set $U$ has quasi-continuous representative zero quasi-everywhere there. The ideal property therefore applies to $K \subset U$. This is a local Dirichlet-space approximation fact and does not require a bounded domain or positive uniform distance between $K$ and $D \setminus U$. Taking orthogonal complements gives

$$ H^\perp(D \setminus K) \subseteq S(U). $$

For the reverse inclusion in the limiting space, take $U_n = \{z \in D : \operatorname{dist}(z, K) < 1/n\}$ and put $S_\infty = \bigcap_n S(U_n)$. Every $v \in C_0^\infty(D \setminus K)$ has compact support disjoint from $U_n$

<!-- page 44 -->

for sufficiently large $n$, so $v \in S(U_n)^\perp$ for those $n$. Consequently $S_\infty \perp H(D \setminus K)$. We have proved

$$ S_\infty = H^\perp(D \setminus K). $$

The empty-set case has the same conclusion directly.

We recall why decreasing closed Gaussian subspaces give the corresponding intersection of sigma-algebras. Let $P_n$ and $P$ be the orthogonal projections onto $S(U_n)$ and $S_\infty$. The projections $P_n$ converge strongly to $P$, and Gaussian orthogonal decomposition gives

$$ \mathbf{E}\big[e^{iX(v)} \mid \sigma(X(S(U_n)))\big] = \exp\left( iX(P_n v) - \frac{1}{2} \|v - P_n v\|_\nabla^2 \right). $$

The right side converges in probability and in $L^1$ to the same expression with $P$ in place of $P_n$. By reverse martingale convergence, the left side converges to conditioning on the intersection of the sigma-algebras. These identities, for a countable dense family of $v$ and their rational linear combinations, determine conditional laws of the whole Gaussian field. They show that

$$ \bigcap_n \sigma(X(S(U_n))) = \sigma(X(S_\infty)) = \mathcal{F}_{K+}^h $$

modulo completion. Finally $H^\perp(D \setminus K) \subseteq S(U)$ for every open $U \supseteq K$, while the intersection over all such $U$ is contained in the intersection over the particular $U_n$. These two inclusions prove the proposition for every domain on which the stated Dirichlet GFF exists. $\square$

<a id="prop-3.3"></a>
**Proposition 3.3.** Assume that $D$ is a non-trivial simply connected domain and let $K$ be a deterministic closed subset of $D$. Let $h_1$ be the harmonic function on $D \setminus K$ which agrees with the projection of $h$ onto $H^\perp(D \setminus K)$, restricted to $D \setminus K$. Then a.s.

$$ \lim_{D \setminus K \ni z \to z_0} h_1(z) = 0 \quad \text{for all} \quad z_0 \in \partial D \setminus \overline{K}. $$

Here the closure of $K$ is taken in $\mathbf{C}$; equivalently, the boundary point under consideration has positive distance from $K$.

<a id="prop-3.3-proof"></a>
*Proof.* The case $K = \varnothing$ is immediate, since then $h_1 = 0$. The assertion is local near the boundary, so fix a bounded open patch $O$ whose closure has positive distance $R$ from $K$, and a smaller patch $O_0$ with $\overline{O}_0 \subset O$. We prove simultaneous boundary convergence in $O_0$; countably many such patches cover $\partial D \setminus \overline{K}$.

The harmonic Gaussian projection $h_1$ has covariance

$$ Q(z, w) = G_D(z, w) - G_{D \setminus K}(z, w), \qquad z, w \in D \setminus K. $$

<!-- page 45 -->

We first bound its diagonal near $\partial D$. Put $d(z) = \operatorname{dist}(z, \partial D)$. Let $f : \mathbf{D} \to D$ be conformal with $f(0) = z$, and write $r = |f'(0)| = \operatorname{crad}(D, z) \le 4d(z)$. If $u = f(\zeta) \in K$, the upper growth estimate for a univalent function gives

$$ R \le |u - z| \le \frac{r|\zeta|}{(1 - |\zeta|)^2}. $$

Consequently $1 - |\zeta| \le 2\sqrt{d(z)/R}$. If $d(z)/R \le 1/16$, then $|\zeta| \ge 1/2$, and conformal invariance of the Green function yields

$$ 0 \le G_D(z, u) = -\log|\zeta| \le 2(1 - |\zeta|) \le 4\sqrt{d(z)/R}. $$

The strong Markov identity for killed Brownian motion, with $\tau_K$ the first hit of $K$ and $\tau_D$ the exit time from $D$, gives

$$ Q(z, z) = \mathbb{E}^z\big[ G_D(B_{\tau_K}, z) \mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_K < \tau_D\}} \big] \le 4\sqrt{d(z)/R}. $$

The diagonal identity follows by first using distinct points in the Green identity and then taking their limit; the separation from $K$ and the preceding bound justify this limit. Thus, for every $p \ge 1$, the Gaussian moment formula implies

$$ \mathbb{E}|h_1(z)|^p \le C_{p,R} \, d(z)^{p/4} $$

throughout the part of $O$ sufficiently close to $\partial D$.

Take a Whitney decomposition of $D$, with square side lengths dyadic and comparable to their distances from $\partial D$. The decomposition may be chosen so that a fixed enlargement of each square is still inside $D$. For every sufficiently small square $Q$ meeting $O_0$, that enlargement lies in $O$ and avoids $K$. The harmonic submean estimate, followed by the last Gaussian moment bound, gives

$$ \mathbb{E}\left[ \sup_{z \in Q} |h_1(z)|^p \right] \le C_{p,R} \, \ell(Q)^{p/4}. $$

Indeed, the supremum on $Q$ is bounded by a constant times $\ell(Q)^{-2}$ times the integral of $|h_1|^p$ on the enlargement, and $d(z)$ is comparable to $\ell(Q)$ there.

There are at most $C_{O_0} \delta^{-2}$ Whitney squares of side $\delta$ meeting the bounded patch. Fix $0 < \beta < 1/4$ and choose $p$ so large that $p(1/4 - \beta) > 2$. Markov's inequality and a union bound show that the probability that any such square has $\sup_Q |h_1| > \delta^\beta$ is at most

$$ C\delta^{p(1/4 - \beta) - 2}. $$

These bounds are summable over dyadic $\delta \downarrow 0$. By Borel–Cantelli, almost surely all sufficiently small Whitney squares meeting $O_0$ satisfy $\sup_Q |h_1| \le \ell(Q)^\beta$. Since their side lengths are comparable to distance from the boundary, this proves uniform convergence to zero as points of $D \cap O_0$ approach $\partial D \cap O_0$. The countable patch covering proves the asserted simultaneous statement for every boundary point in $\partial D \setminus \overline{K}$. $\square$

We finish this subsection with the following proposition, which describes the absolute continuity properties of the GFF.

<a id="prop-3.4"></a>
**Proposition 3.4** (Absolute Continuity). Suppose that $D_1, D_2$ are simply connected domains with $D_1 \cap D_2 \ne \emptyset$. For $i = 1, 2$, let $h_i$ be a zero boundary GFF on $D_i$ and $F_i$ harmonic on $D_i$. Fix a bounded simply connected open domain $U \subseteq D_1 \cap D_2$.

(i) (Interior) If $\operatorname{dist}(U, \partial D_i) > 0$ for $i = 1, 2$ then the laws of $(h_1 + F_1)|_U$ and $(h_2 + F_2)|_U$ are mutually absolutely continuous.

(ii) (Boundary) Suppose that there is a neighborhood $U'$ of the closure $\overline{U}$ of $U$ such that $D_1 \cap U' = D_2 \cap U'$, and that $F_1 - F_2$ tends to zero as one approaches points in the sets $\partial D_i \cap U'$. Then the laws of $(h_1 + F_1)|_U$ and $(h_2 + F_2)|_U$ are mutually absolutely continuous.

Although Proposition 3.4 is stated in the case that $U$ is bounded, an analogous result holds when $U$ is not bounded. We will in particular use this result in the following setting without reference: $D_1 = D_2 = D$ and there exists a conformal transformation $\varphi \colon D \to \widetilde{D}$ where $\widetilde{D}$ is bounded and $\widetilde{U} = \varphi(U)$ satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition 3.4 part (i) or part (ii).

We will often make use of Proposition 3.4 in the following manner. Theorem 1.1 gives the existence of a coupling of an $\mathrm{SLE}_\kappa(\underline{\rho})$ (a flow line) or $\mathrm{SLE}_{\kappa'}(\underline{\rho})$ process (a counterflow line) with the GFF (say, on $\mathbf{H}$) provided the boundary data of the GFF is piecewise constant and changes values a finite number of times. For more general boundary data, one can still construct the flow and counterflow lines of the GFF provided the boundary data near the starting point is piecewise constant. The reason for this is that Proposition 3.4 implies that the law of the field near the starting point is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of a GFF that does have piecewise constant boundary data which changes a finite number of times.

More precisely, suppose that $h$ is a GFF on $\mathbf{H}$ with piecewise constant boundary data and that $\eta$ is its flow or counterflow line starting from $x \in \partial\mathbf{H}$. If $\widetilde{h}$ is another GFF on $\mathbf{H}$ whose boundary data agrees with that of $h$ in a neighborhood of $x$ and $U$ is any bounded, open subset of $\overline{\mathbf{H}}$ which contains a neighborhood of $x$ and with positive distance from those boundary segments where the boundary data of $h$ and $\widetilde{h}$ differ, then we know that the laws of $h|_U$ and $\widetilde{h}|_U$ are mutually absolutely continuous. Let $Z_U$ denote the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the law of the latter with respect to the former. Then weighting the law of $(h|_U, \eta_U)$, $\eta_U$ given by $\eta$ stopped upon first exiting $U$, by $Z_U$ yields a coupling where the marginal law of the first element is the same as the law of $\widetilde{h}|_U$, the marginal law of the second element is mutually absolutely continuous with respect to the law of $\eta_U$, and the pair satisfies the same Markov property described in the statement of Theorem 1.1. (We will justify the Markov property more carefully just after the proof of Lemma 3.6.)

Note that all of the almost sure properties of $\eta_U$ are preserved under this change of measure. The caveat is that by combining Theorem 1.1 and Proposition 3.4 in this way (with larger and larger domains $U$), the flow or counterflow line of $\widetilde{h}$ will only be defined up until it hits a boundary segment which does not have piecewise constant boundary data.

One can also apply this operation in reverse. Namely, suppose that $h$ is a GFF on $\mathbf{H}$, $U \subseteq \mathbf{H}$ is bounded and open such that $h$ has piecewise constant boundary data in $\partial\mathbf{H} \cap \overline{U}$, and $\eta$ is a path coupled with $h$ which satisfies the Markov property described in the statement of Theorem 1.1. Then Proposition 3.4 and Theorem 2.4 together imply that the joint law of $(h|_U, \eta_U)$, with $\eta_U$ as defined above, is mutually absolutely continuous with respect to the joint law of a GFF with piecewise constant boundary data on all of $\partial\mathbf{H}$ and its flow or counterflow line starting from the origin, both restricted to $U$. In particular, all of the almost sure properties of $\eta_U$ are the same under both laws. This is useful because the Loewner driving function for $\eta_U$ may not be described by a simple SDE.

*Proof of Proposition 3.4.* Let $\widetilde{U} \subseteq D_1 \cap D_2$ be such that $\operatorname{dist}(\widetilde{U}, \partial D_i) > 0$ for $i = 1, 2$, $U \subseteq \widetilde{U}$, and $\operatorname{dist}(U, \partial\widetilde{U}) > 0$. By Proposition 3.1, we can write $(h_i + F_i)|_{\widetilde{U}} = h_i^{\widetilde{U}} + (h_i^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_i)|_{\widetilde{U}}$ where $h_i^{\widetilde{U}}$ is a zero boundary GFF on $\widetilde{U}$ and $h_i^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_i$ is harmonic on $\widetilde{U}$, both restricted to $\widetilde{U}$. We assume that $h_1, h_2$ are coupled together on a common probability space so that $h_1^{\widetilde{U}} = h_2^{\widetilde{U}}$ and that $h_1^{\widetilde{U}^c}, h_2^{\widetilde{U}^c}$ are independent. Let $\phi \in C_0^\infty(\widetilde{U})$ be such that $\phi|_U \equiv 1$ and let $g = \phi\left( (h_2^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_2) - (h_1^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_1) \right)$. Then we have that $g \in H(\widetilde{U})$ and $(h_1 + F_1 + g)|_U = (h_2 + F_2)|_U$. We note that $g$ is a measurable function of $h_1, h_2$ since the $h_i^{\widetilde{U}^c}$ for $i = 1, 2$ are measurable functions of $h_1, h_2$ as their series expansions can be determined by taking a $(\cdot,\cdot)_\nabla$-inner product of $h_1, h_2$ with respect to an appropriately chosen orthonormal basis. Similarly, $(h_1^{\widetilde{U}}, g)_\nabla$ is a measurable function of $h_1, h_2$ because we can represent it as $\sum_j a_j b_j$ where $(a_j)$, $(b_j)$ are the coordinates of $h_1^{\widetilde{U}}, g$ with respect to a $(\cdot,\cdot)_\nabla$-orthonormal basis. We note that, although $g$ is a random function in $H(\widetilde{U})$, the series $\sum_j a_j b_j$ converges almost surely because $g$ is independent of $h_1^{\widetilde{U}}$. (Here, we are using that this holds if $g$ is any fixed function in $H(\widetilde{U})$ hence also holds in the case that $g$ is independent of $h_1^{\widetilde{U}}$ by Fubini's theorem.) If we weight the law of $h_1, h_2$ by $\mathcal{Z}^{-1} \exp((h_1^{\widetilde{U}}, g)_\nabla)$ where $\mathcal{Z} = \exp(\frac{1}{2}\|g\|_\nabla^2)$ then the law of $h_1^{\widetilde{U}}$ under the weighted law is equal to the law of $h_1^{\widetilde{U}} + g$ under the unweighted

law. As $g$ is compactly supported in $\widetilde{U}$ and $h_1^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_1$ is harmonic in $\widetilde{U}$, we have that $(h_1^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_1, g)_\nabla = 0$, hence $(h_1^{\widetilde{U}}, g)_\nabla = (h_1 + F_1, g)_\nabla$. Therefore if we weight the law of $h_1, h_2$ by $\mathcal{Z}^{-1} \exp((h_1 + F_1, g)_\nabla)$ where $\mathcal{Z} = \exp(\frac{1}{2}\|g\|_\nabla^2)$ then the law of $(h_1 + F_1)|_U$ under the weighted law is equal to the law of $(h_1 + F_1 + g)|_U = (h_2 + F_2)|_U$ under the unweighted law. We conclude that, under the coupling that we have constructed of $h_1, h_2$, an event for $(h_1 + F_1)|_U$ has zero probability if and only if it has zero probability for $(h_2 + F_2)|_U$. That is, the law of $(h_1 + F_1)|_U$ is mutually absolutely continuous with respect to the law of $(h_2 + F_2)|_U$. This proves part (i).

For part (ii), we let $\widetilde{U} = U' \cap D_1 = U' \cap D_2$. Then we have $(h_i + F_i)|_{\widetilde{U}} = h_i^{\widetilde{U}} + (h_i^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_i)|_{\widetilde{U}}$ where $h_i^{\widetilde{U}}$ is a zero boundary GFF on $\widetilde{U}$ and $h_i^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_i$ is harmonic in $\widetilde{U}$, both restricted to $\widetilde{U}$. We assume that $h_1, h_2$ are coupled on a common probability space so that $h_1^{\widetilde{U}} = h_2^{\widetilde{U}}$ and $h_1^{\widetilde{U}^c}, h_2^{\widetilde{U}^c}$ are independent. Choose $\phi \in C_0^\infty(U')$ with $\phi|_U \equiv 1$ and let $g = \phi\left( (h_2^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_2) - (h_1^{\widetilde{U}^c} + F_1) \right)$. Then we have that $(h_1 + F_1 + g)|_U = (h_2 + F_2)|_U$ where $g \in H(\widetilde{U})$.

For clarity, finite Dirichlet energy at the genuine common boundary does not follow just from interior smoothness. The harmonic difference in parentheses extends continuously as zero there: the deterministic part does so by hypothesis, and the two harmonic GFF projections have zero boundary behavior locally away from the artificial boundary, by Proposition 3.3. It is therefore bounded on the compact support of a slightly larger cutoff. If $u$ denotes this harmonic difference and $\zeta$ is such a larger cutoff, testing harmonicity with $\zeta^2((u - \epsilon)_+ - (-u - \epsilon)_+)$ gives

$$ \int_{\{|u| > \epsilon\}} \zeta^2 |\nabla u|^2 \le 4 \int u^2 |\nabla \zeta|^2. $$

The test is compactly supported inside the domain because the relevant boundary values are zero. Letting $\epsilon \downarrow 0$, and approximating by these truncated functions, proves that the cutoff shift $g$ has finite energy and zero Sobolev boundary values, as required for the Cameron–Martin argument.

The remaining change-of-measure argument is the one used for part (i). $\square$
